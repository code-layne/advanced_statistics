  \item \ldots{} find the average of a set of values and the value sitting in the middle of it,
        and say what it means when the two disagree.
  \item \ldots{} show how one unusual value can drag one of those summaries a long way while the
        other does not move at all.
  \item \ldots{} put a single number on how spread out a set of values is --- and say what that
        number can be fooled by.
  \item \ldots{} decide which summary to report, and defend the decision from the shape of the
        picture.
\end{enumerate}

\boxguard[8]
\begin{remindbox}
\small
In this lesson you will \textbf{experience first, formalize later}: your group gets nine
paychecks from one small company and two delivery routes' worth of times, and works out what a
single summary number can honestly claim about a group of people --- only afterward will we name
the summaries. Two of your answers will be \emph{\$250 apart and both correct}. Figuring out how
that is possible, and which one you would be willing to print, is the whole point.
\end{remindbox}
