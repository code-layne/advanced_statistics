\begin{headlinebox}{sky}
\textbf{Two Checkout Lines.} A supermarket timed 20 customers in each of its two checkout
lines. Both lines have \emph{exactly the same} middle wait. Work the two problems with your
group using only what you already know.
\end{headlinebox}

\vspace{0.02in}

Each dot is one customer. Both plots use the same scale, so you can compare them directly.

\vspace{3pt}

\normalsize
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\alph*.}, leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=4pt]
  \item How many customers were timed in Line A? \ans{20} \quad In Line B? \ans{20}

        \vspace{3pt}
        Counting in from both ends, the middle wait in Line A is \ans{4} minutes and the
        middle wait in Line B is \ans{4} minutes.
  \item Both lines have the same middle wait. \textbf{Which line would you rather join, and
        why?} Point at customers on the plots, not at the middle number.
        \answerspace{1.6cm}{Line A. All 20 of its customers were through in 3 to 6 minutes, so
        there is no bad outcome. Line B gets four people out in 1 minute but leaves two waiting
        11 and 13.}
  \item The manager reports one sentence to head office: \emph{``The typical wait is 4 minutes
        in both lines.''} Say exactly what that sentence hides about Line B.
        \answerspace{1.6cm}{It hides how far apart Line B's waits are: Line A never leaves 3 to
        6 minutes, while Line B runs from 1 minute to 13 and strands two customers past 11.}
  \item Two Line B customers sit far to the right of everybody else, at \textbf{11} and
        \textbf{13} minutes. What would make one of those a recording mistake? What would make
        it a real customer? Should it be left in the data either way?
        \answerspace{2.0cm}{A mistake if the timer was left running or the wait went into the
        wrong row --- something you could check. Real if that customer hit a price check or a
        very full cart. Unless you can show it was an error it stays: you report an odd value,
        you do not delete it.}
\end{enumerate}
\end{scenariobox}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\alph*.}, leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=4pt]
  \item For each picture: where do most of the values pile up, and which way do the stragglers
        run?

        \vspace{3pt}
        \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
        \begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} c X X @{}}
        \rowcolor{sky}
        \textbf{Picture} & \textbf{Most of the values sit\ldots} &
        \textbf{The few stragglers run toward\ldots} \\
        \hline
        \textbf{I} & \ans{at the low end} & \ans{the high end} \\
        \textbf{II} & \ans{at the high end} & \ans{the low end} \\
        \textbf{III} & \ans{in two humps} & \ans{neither side} \\
        \end{tabularx}
  \item Two of these three pictures lean, and they lean in \emph{opposite} directions. In your
        own words, describe how those two differ from each other.
        \answerspace{1.6cm}{In I the pile is at the cheap end and the strays run to the
        expensive end; in II the pile is at the high scores and the strays run down to the low
        ones. Mirror images.}
  \item Picture III has two humps with an empty stretch between them. Suppose somebody told you
        only \emph{``The typical height in this club is about 67 inches.''} What would that tell
        you about the club --- and what would it leave out?
        \answerspace{1.8cm}{Almost nothing true --- nobody in the club is near 67 inches; that
        stretch is empty. The club is two groups, one near 63 inches and one near 73, and the
        number fits neither.}
  \item A student summarized all three pictures by reporting only the average. Say what that
        summary misses in each case. Then write \textbf{one sentence} about Picture II that a
        person who had never seen it could still trust.
        \answerspace{2.2cm}{I hides the few very expensive homes; II hides the students who did
        badly on an easy quiz; III lands in the empty stretch and fits nobody.\\[2pt]
        Picture II: the 32 quiz scores bunch high, most between 80 and 100, with a few
        trailing down toward 50.}
\end{enumerate}

\begin{tcolorbox}[enhanced, colback=sky, colframe=navy, boxrule=0.8pt, arc=2mm,
    left=3mm, right=3mm, top=1.5mm, bottom=1.5mm, breakable,
    fonttitle=\bfseries\small\color{white}, title={QuickNotes: Describing a Quantitative Distribution},
    attach boxed title to top left={yshift=-2mm, xshift=4mm},
    boxed title style={colback=navy, colframe=navy, arc=1.5mm}]
\small
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, itemsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{Shape.} \textbf{Skewed right:} the \ans{right} tail is longer.
        \textbf{Skewed left:} the \ans{left} tail is longer. \textbf{Symmetric:} the left
        half is roughly a \ans{mirror image} of the right.
        \textbf{The name follows the \ans{tail}, not the \ans{pile}.}
  \item \textbf{Peaks.} One main peak: \ans{unimodal}. Two prominent peaks: \ans{bimodal}.
        All bars about equal, no peak: \ans{uniform}.
  \item \textbf{Center} --- the \ans{typical} value. \textbf{Variability (spread)} --- how far
        the values \ans{are spread out}. Line A and Line B: same center, different spread.
  \item \textbf{Unusual features.} \textbf{Outlier:} a value unusually small or large
        \ans{relative to} the rest of the data. \textbf{Gap:} a stretch where \ans{no data} was
        observed. \textbf{Cluster:} values concentrated together, set off by \ans{gaps}.
  \item \textbf{Four things or nothing:} \textbf{S}hape, \textbf{O}utliers, \textbf{C}enter,
        \textbf{S}pread --- remember it as \ans{SOCS} --- and every one stated
        \ans{in context}, naming the variable and its units.
  \item Describing \emph{these} data is not a claim about a larger \ans{population}; it can only
        suggest something worth \ans{testing} later.
\end{itemize}
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{notesbox}{Application: The Sleep Survey}
We will do this one together. Thirty students reported their hours of sleep on a school night.

\vspace{3pt}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.80]
  \draw[->, thick] (-0.2,0) -- (8.2,0);
  \foreach \x/\lab in {0.6/4, 1.8/5, 3.0/6, 4.2/7, 5.4/8, 6.6/9, 7.8/10} {
    \draw (\x,-0.07) -- (\x,0.07); \node[below, font=\scriptsize] at (\x,-0.1) {\lab};}
  \foreach \x/\n in {0.6/1, 3.0/1, 3.6/2, 4.2/4, 4.8/5, 5.4/7, 6.0/6, 6.6/3, 7.2/1} {
    \foreach \k in {1,...,\n} { \fill[navy] (\x,{0.2*\k}) circle (0.07); }}
  \node[font=\scriptsize] at (4.0,-0.62) {Hours of sleep on a school night};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\vspace{2pt}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\alph*.}, leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=4pt]
  \item How many students are shown? \ans{30} \quad One value sits apart from all the
        others: \ans{4} hours.
  \item Which way does this picture lean? \ans{to the left} \quad Say what that longer tail means
        \emph{for these students} --- not for the graph.
        \answerspace{1.6cm}{The tail runs toward the low end, so a few of these students get far
        less sleep than the rest --- one of them only 4 hours on a school night.}
  \item Now write the complete description in one paragraph: all four parts, in context, with
        the variable and its units named.
        \answerspace{2.4cm}{These 30 students' hours of sleep are unimodal and skewed to the
        left. The center is about 8 hours a night, and values run from 4 to 9.5 hours, with most
        between 7 and 9. One student reported only 4 hours, far below the rest and cut off by an
        empty stretch.}
\end{enumerate}
\end{notesbox}
