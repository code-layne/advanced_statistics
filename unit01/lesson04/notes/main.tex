\boxguard[12]
\begin{notesbox}{1. Center, Variability, and the Values That Sit Apart}
\small
Last lesson you learned to \emph{read} these displays. Today you learn to \emph{say what they
show} --- and one number is never enough.

\vspace{5pt}
A supermarket timed 20 customers in each of its two checkout lines. Both plots use the same
scale, so you can compare them directly.

\vspace{3pt}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.80]
  % ── Line A: tightly bunched ──
  \draw[->, thick] (-0.2,0) -- (6.0,0);
  \foreach \x/\lab in {0/0, 0.7/2, 1.4/4, 2.1/6, 2.8/8, 3.5/10, 4.2/12, 4.9/14, 5.6/16} {
    \draw (\x,-0.07) -- (\x,0.07); \node[below, font=\tiny] at (\x,-0.1) {\lab};}
  \foreach \x/\n in {1.05/4, 1.4/8, 1.75/5, 2.1/3} {
    \foreach \k in {1,...,\n} { \fill[navy] (\x,{0.2*\k}) circle (0.07); }}
  \node[font=\tiny] at (2.8,-0.85) {Minutes waited};
  \node[font=\bfseries\small] at (2.8,2.15) {Line A};
  % ── Line B: widely spread, two customers stranded ──
  \begin{scope}[xshift=7.0cm]
    \draw[->, thick] (-0.2,0) -- (6.0,0);
    \foreach \x/\lab in {0/0, 0.7/2, 1.4/4, 2.1/6, 2.8/8, 3.5/10, 4.2/12, 4.9/14, 5.6/16} {
      \draw (\x,-0.07) -- (\x,0.07); \node[below, font=\tiny] at (\x,-0.1) {\lab};}
    \foreach \x/\n in {0.35/4, 0.7/3, 1.05/2, 1.4/2, 1.75/3, 2.1/2, 2.45/2, 3.85/1, 4.55/1} {
      \foreach \k in {1,...,\n} { \fill[goldacc] (\x,{0.2*\k}) circle (0.07); }}
    \node[font=\tiny] at (2.8,-0.85) {Minutes waited};
    \node[font=\bfseries\small] at (2.8,2.15) {Line B};
  \end{scope}
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Read them.} Counting in from both ends, the middle wait in Line A is \blank{1.1cm}
minutes and the middle wait in Line B is \blank{1.1cm} minutes.

\vspace{5pt}
The \textbf{center} of a distribution is that middle, typical value --- where the data
\blank{1.5cm} up. Both lines have the \blank{1.2cm} center. The \textbf{variability}, or
\textbf{spread}, is how far the values reach \blank{2.6cm}. In Line A every wait falls between
\blank{0.9cm} and \blank{0.9cm} minutes; in Line B they run from \blank{0.9cm} all the way to
\blank{0.9cm}.

\vspace{5pt}
\begin{center}
\fcolorbox{redacc}{redbg}{\parbox{0.94\linewidth}{\small
\textbf{Careful --- one number is not a description.} The manager reports
\emph{``the typical wait is 4 minutes in both lines.''} That sentence is \blank{1.3cm} of both
lines and tells a customer \blank{1.6cm} about which one to join. A center reported without the
\blank{1.8cm} beside it is a summary of nothing.}}
\end{center}

\vspace{10pt}
\textbf{\textcolor{navy}{What Sits Apart: Outliers, Gaps, and Clusters.}}
Line B is not just wider than Line A --- two of its customers are somewhere else entirely.

\vspace{4pt}
An \textbf{outlier} is a value that sits unusually far \blank{1.3cm} the rest of the data. In
Line B the customers at \blank{0.9cm} and \blank{0.9cm} minutes are outliers. A \textbf{gap} is a
stretch of the number line where \blank{1.6cm} was observed --- here, everything from about 7
minutes to 11. A \textbf{cluster} is a group of values bunched together and set off from the
other values by a \blank{1.0cm}.

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{An outlier is not automatically a mistake.} Check it, do not \blank{1.5cm} it. If the
13-minute customer is a typing error, fix the record; if she is a real person with a full cart
and a price check, she is precisely the customer the manager needs to hear about.
\end{notesbox}

\begin{notesbox}{2. Shape --- and the Tail That Names It}
\small
Three different things were measured, each drawn as a histogram. \textbf{I} --- sale prices of
the 40 homes sold in one town last year. \textbf{II} --- scores of 32 students on a quiz most of
the class found easy. \textbf{III} --- heights of the 40 adults in a mixed volleyball club.

\vspace{3pt}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.78]
  % ── I: home prices, long right tail ──
  \foreach \i/\c in {0/6, 1/14, 2/10, 3/5, 4/3, 5/1, 6/1} {
    \fill[navy] ({\i*0.5},0) rectangle ({(\i+1)*0.5},{\c*0.14});
    \draw[white, very thin] ({\i*0.5},0) rectangle ({(\i+1)*0.5},{\c*0.14});}
  \draw[->, thick] (0,0) -- (0,2.5);
  \draw[->, thick] (0,0) -- (3.9,0);
  \foreach \y/\lab in {0/0, 0.7/5, 1.4/10} {
    \draw (-0.07,\y) -- (0.07,\y); \node[left, font=\tiny, xshift=-1pt] at (-0.07,\y) {\lab};}
  \foreach \x/\lab in {0/100, 1/300, 2/500, 3/700} {
    \draw (\x,-0.07) -- (\x,0.07); \node[below, font=\tiny] at (\x,-0.1) {\lab};}
  \node[font=\tiny] at (1.75,-0.85) {Sale price (\$1000s)};
  \node[font=\bfseries\small] at (1.75,2.75) {I};

  \begin{scope}[xshift=4.9cm]

    \draw[->, thick] (0,0) -- (0,2.5);
    \draw[->, thick] (0,0) -- (3.9,0);
    \foreach \y/\lab in {0/0, 0.7/5, 1.4/10} {
      \draw (-0.07,\y) -- (0.07,\y); \node[left, font=\tiny, xshift=-1pt] at (-0.07,\y) {\lab};}
    \foreach \x/\lab in {0/50, 1.4/70, 2.8/90} {

    \node[font=\bfseries\small] at (1.75,2.75) {II};
  \end{scope}
  % ── III: club heights, symmetric with two humps and an empty stretch ──
  \begin{scope}[xshift=9.8cm]
    \foreach \i/\c in {0/2, 1/5, 2/9, 3/4, 4/0, 5/0, 6/4, 7/9, 8/5, 9/2} {
      \fill[navy] ({\i*0.35},0) rectangle ({(\i+1)*0.35},{\c*0.14});
      \draw[white, very thin] ({\i*0.35},0) rectangle ({(\i+1)*0.35},{\c*0.14});}
    \draw[->, thick] (0,0) -- (0,2.5);
    \draw[->, thick] (0,0) -- (3.9,0);
    \foreach \y/\lab in {0/0, 0.7/5, 1.4/10} {
      \draw (-0.07,\y) -- (0.07,\y); \node[left, font=\tiny, xshift=-1pt] at (-0.07,\y) {\lab};}
    \foreach \x/\lab in {0/58, 1.4/66, 2.8/74} {
      \draw (\x,-0.07) -- (\x,0.07); \node[below, font=\tiny] at (\x,-0.1) {\lab};}
    \node[font=\tiny] at (1.75,-0.85) {Height (inches)};
    \node[font=\bfseries\small] at (1.75,2.75) {III};
  \end{scope}
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\vspace{4pt}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} c X X @{}}
\rowcolor{sky}
\textbf{Picture} & \textbf{Most of the values sit\ldots} &
\textbf{The few stragglers run toward\ldots} \\
\hline
\textbf{I} & \blank{3.6cm} & \blank{3.6cm} \\
\textbf{II} & \blank{3.6cm} & \blank{3.6cm} \\
\textbf{III} & \blank{3.6cm} & \blank{3.6cm} \\
\end{tabularx}

\vspace{6pt}
\textbf{Count the peaks first.} One main peak: \blank{2.0cm}. Two clear peaks: \blank{1.9cm}.
All the bars about equal, with no peak at all: \blank{1.8cm}.

\vspace{4pt}
A distribution is \textbf{symmetric} when the left half is roughly a \blank{2.4cm} of the right.
Picture \blank{0.8cm} is symmetric; Pictures I and II are not, because each one \blank{1.4cm}.

\vspace{10pt}
\textbf{\textcolor{navy}{Skewed Which Way? The Tail Decides.}}
Look again at Picture I. Count the homes in the bars:

\begin{work}
\text{Homes that sold below \$400{,}000} &= 6 + 14 + 10 = 30 \text{ of the } 40 \\[4pt]
\text{Homes that sold above \$600{,}000} &= 1 + 1 = 2 \text{ of the } 40
\end{work}

Thirty of the forty homes sold for under \$400{,}000, and the picture still stretches out past
\$700{,}000 --- because two houses really did sell for that. The pile is on the \blank{1.0cm};
the long thin tail runs to the \blank{1.2cm}.

\vspace{4pt}
We call Picture I \textbf{skewed right}. Read that again: the data is piled on the \emph{left},
and the name says \emph{right}.

\vspace{5pt}
\begin{center}
\fcolorbox{navy}{sky}{\parbox{0.92\linewidth}{\centering\small
\textbf{The name of a skew follows the \blank{1.1cm}, not the \blank{1.1cm}.}\\
Skewed \textbf{right}: the longer tail runs right, so most values are \blank{1.4cm} and a few
are much larger. Skewed \textbf{left}: the longer tail runs \blank{1.1cm}.}}
\end{center}

\vspace{5pt}
Picture II is the mirror image. The quiz was easy, so most scores pile up on the
\blank{1.0cm}, and the few students who struggled trail off to the left. Picture II is skewed
\blank{1.0cm} --- and saying \emph{``skewed right, because the bars on the right are tallest''}
gets it exactly backwards.

\vspace{6pt}
\textbf{Symmetric does not mean ``nothing to report.''} Picture III is symmetric, and it is also
\blank{1.8cm}, with a gap between about \blank{0.9cm} and \blank{0.9cm} inches. Somebody who
reports only \emph{``the typical height in this club is about 67 inches''} has said something
true of the picture and false of every \blank{1.5cm} in it: the club is roughly half shorter
adults and half taller ones, and \blank{2.2cm} of them is 67 inches tall. Shape is never one
word, and center is never the whole answer.

\vspace{6pt}
\begin{center}
\fcolorbox{navy}{sky}{\parbox{0.92\linewidth}{\centering\small
\textbf{Four things or nothing --- \blank{1.5cm}}\\
\textbf{S}hape \quad$\cdot$\quad \textbf{O}utliers and other unusual features \quad$\cdot$\quad
\textbf{C}enter \quad$\cdot$\quad \textbf{S}pread\\
and every one of the four stated \blank{2.0cm}, naming the variable and its units.}}
\end{center}

\vspace{5pt}
\textbf{One limit on what you may claim.} Describing these 40 homes is a statement about
\blank{1.5cm} 40 homes. It is not a claim about houses in general; at most it \blank{1.9cm}
something worth investigating with data collected for that purpose.
\end{notesbox}
