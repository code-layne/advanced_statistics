\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=sky, colframe=navy, boxrule=0.9pt, arc=2mm,
    left=2mm, right=2mm, top=1.5mm, bottom=1.5mm]
    \textbf{Primary Objective:} Students will describe the distribution of a quantitative
    variable by naming its shape, center, and variability, together with any unusual features
    --- outliers, gaps, clusters, or multiple peaks --- and will state every one of those
    four things in the context of the data.
    \\[2pt]
    \textbf{CED alignment:} Topic 1.6 \quad$\cdot$\quad Big Idea: UNC (Uncertainty)
    \quad$\cdot$\quad Skill 2.A \quad$\cdot$\quad LO UNC-1.H \quad$\cdot$\quad
    EK UNC-1.H.1--1.H.7
    \\[2pt]
    \textbf{Lesson model:} \emph{Experience first, formalize later.} Students work the activity
    in small groups using only prior knowledge; the teacher circulates with questions, cues, and
    prompts (not answers); the debrief attaches the formal vocabulary to what the groups already
    found. \textbf{No vocabulary before the debrief.}
    \\[2pt]
    \textbf{Note on the ``no sketch'' rule:} every display in this lesson is \textbf{given}.
    Students read, compare, and describe --- they never draw an axis. Describing is the skill
    the exam scores (Skill 2.A); drawing is not, and it eats the period.
\end{tcolorbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Learning Targets \& Key Understandings]{goldbox}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X|X@{}}
\textbf{Learning targets (student language)} & \textbf{Key understandings (the ``why'')} \\[2pt]
\hline
\vspace{-6pt}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, nosep, after=\vspace{2pt}]
  \item I can say why the typical value alone does not describe a set of data.
  \item I can name the shape of a distribution, including which way it leans.
  \item I can point out values that sit apart, empty stretches, and separate groups.
  \item I can write a complete description of a display, in context.
\end{itemize}
&
\vspace{-6pt}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, nosep, after=\vspace{2pt}]
  \item A description of quantitative data needs \textbf{shape, center, variability, and
        unusual features} --- all four, all in context (EK UNC-1.H.1).
  \item Skew is named for the \textbf{tail}, not the pile: right tail longer $\to$ skewed
        right (EK UNC-1.H.3).
  \item \textbf{Target misconception:} a picture whose values are bunched on the left gets
        called ``skewed left'' because students name the picture by where the data \emph{is}.
        The convention names it by where the \emph{stragglers go} (EK UNC-1.H.3).
  \item \textbf{Second misconception:} that describing these data describes a population.
        It does not --- it can only suggest a conjecture to test later (EK UNC-1.H.7).
\end{itemize}
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[{Vocabulary, Concepts, \& Theorems --- attached in the debrief, not before}]{greenbox}
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.48\linewidth}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Shape} --- symmetric, skewed left (negative), skewed right (positive)
              (EK UNC-1.H.3)
        \item \textbf{Unimodal / bimodal / uniform} --- one main peak, two prominent peaks,
              or bars of roughly equal height (EK UNC-1.H.4)
        \item \textbf{Center} --- the typical or middle value of the distribution
              (EK UNC-1.H.1)
        \item \textbf{Variability (spread)} --- how far the values reach from one another
              (EK UNC-1.H.1)
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.48\linewidth}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Outlier} --- a value unusually small or large relative to the rest
              (EK UNC-1.H.2)
        \item \textbf{Gap} --- a stretch between two values where nothing was observed
              (EK UNC-1.H.5)
        \item \textbf{Cluster} --- a concentration of values, usually set off by gaps
              (EK UNC-1.H.6)
        \item \textbf{SOCS} --- shape, outliers/unusual features, center, spread; the
              four-part description, always in context (EK UNC-1.H.1)
        \item \textbf{Carried forward} --- quantitative variables and distribution (1.1);
              dotplots and histograms, and the interval-width warning (1.3)
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Warm-Up --- Activate Prior Knowledge]{sky}
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.48\linewidth}
    \textbf{The three items and what each seeds}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{1.} Two histograms of the same 40 quiz scores. \textbf{Spirals} Lesson 1.3
              --- and installs the habit of checking the interval width before believing a
              shape, which AP Practice item~4 and the free response return to.
        \item \textbf{2.} Nine waits, already in order; count to the middle.
              \textbf{Seeds:} the exact move problem~1a asks for, done once with easy numbers.
        \item \textbf{3.} One pre-drawn plot with a lone dot far out. \textbf{Seeds:} tail
              language --- ``where the rest of them are'' --- in words students already own.
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.48\linewidth}
    \textbf{Running it}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Name nothing.} Do not say \emph{shape}, \emph{skewed}, \emph{center},
              \emph{spread}, \emph{outlier}, \emph{gap}, or \emph{cluster} in the warm-up
              debrief. Write their own phrasing on the board (``one guy way out there,''
              ``they're all bunched up'') and leave it up --- you will relabel it in the
              debrief.
        \item Item~2 is mechanical. Ninety seconds. It exists so that finding the middle is
              never the obstacle inside the activity.
        \item Debrief item~3 aloud and \textbf{write the students' own sentence verbatim}. In
              the debrief you will point at that sentence and say ``that is what the word
              \emph{skewed} names.'' It is far more convincing in their handwriting.
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Experience \& Formalize --- The Activity: \emph{Two Checkout Lines}]{sky}
\textbf{Launch (2 min).} \emph{``Two checkout lines, twenty customers timed in each. Both lines
have the same middle wait. Your job is to decide which line you would stand in --- and then to
say what the manager's one number failed to tell you.''} Groups of 3--4. Do not preview any
vocabulary.

\begin{multicols}{2}
\textbf{What students do.} \emph{Problem 1} is where center collapses as a summary. 1a is
counting and counting to the middle (both medians are 4 minutes), \textbf{1b is the discovery}
--- the same typical wait, two completely different experiences --- 1c forces them to say what
the manager's sentence hides, and 1d asks whether the customers out at 11 and 13 minutes are
errors or real, and whether they should be removed.

\emph{Problem 2} is the crux. Three displays: home prices with a long right tail, an easy quiz
with a long left tail, and a volleyball club that is symmetric with two humps and an empty
stretch between them. 2a makes them say \emph{where the pile is and which way the stragglers
run}, 2b makes them articulate the difference between the two leaning pictures --- which is
exactly the naming convention, arrived at before the name --- and \textbf{2c is the crux}: a
single typical height of about 67 inches belongs to nobody in that club. 2d demands one
trustworthy sentence, and that sentence becomes the QuickNotes structure.

\columnbreak
\textbf{What the teacher does.} Circulate. Listen before speaking. When a group stalls, give a
\emph{question, cue, or prompt}, not an answer:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, nosep]
  \item \textbf{1b:} ``You both said 4 minutes. Now cover the number and look at the picture.
        Which line has a customer having a bad day?''
  \item \textbf{1d:} ``What would have to be true for 13 minutes to be a typo? What would have
        to be true for it to be a real person? Which is easier to believe here?''
  \item \textbf{2a:} ``Put your finger on the tallest bar. Now walk your finger away from it in
        both directions --- which way do you get further before you run out of data?''
  \item \textbf{2c (the crux):} ``Point at the club member who is 67 inches tall.''
\end{itemize}
While circulating, pick the work to display: one group's 1c sentence, and one group's 2b
sentence describing how the two leaning pictures differ --- open the debrief on that one.
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Debrief --- Formalize (what goes on the board, in a second color)]{sky}
Work through the displayed student work in order and \textbf{attach each formal name to
something a group already wrote}. Students fill their QuickNotes line by line as you go.
\begin{multicols}{2}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.3em, nosep, itemsep=3pt]
  \item 1b and 1c $\to$ \emph{center is not enough}. Both lines: middle wait 4 minutes. Write
        both numbers on the board, then write beside them what the two lines actually felt
        like. That contrast is the reason the rest of the vocabulary exists.
  \item Their 2b sentence $\to$ \emph{skewed right} and \emph{skewed left} (EK UNC-1.H.3).
        \textbf{Say the rule out loud and write it: the name follows the tail, not the pile.}
        Then \emph{symmetric} on the volleyball picture, and \emph{unimodal / bimodal /
        uniform} (EK UNC-1.H.4) on all three at once.
  \item 1d $\to$ \emph{outlier} (EK UNC-1.H.2). Take the CED wording exactly: unusually small
        or large \emph{relative to the rest of the data}. Settle the removal question here ---
        you do not delete a value because it is inconvenient.
  \item 2c $\to$ \emph{gap} (EK UNC-1.H.5) and \emph{cluster} (EK UNC-1.H.6). Point at the
        empty stretch between 66 and 70 inches while you say both words.
  \item 2d $\to$ the four-part description and \textbf{SOCS} (EK UNC-1.H.1). Build it out of
        their own trustworthy sentence: whatever they wrote, ask which of the four it is
        missing.
  \item One line, last: EK UNC-1.H.7. Describing these 40 homes is not a claim about every
        town. It can only \emph{suggest} something worth testing.
\end{enumerate}
\columnbreak
\textbf{QuickNotes.} Half a page, filled together. \textbf{It is a summary of what they found,
not a new lecture}; if you are talking for more than 90 seconds without pointing at student
work, stop.

\textbf{Why this order.} Center dies first (otherwise students never feel the need for the rest);
shape second, because that is the naming convention and the target misconception; unusual
features third, on the two displays that already bothered them; and the four-part structure last,
assembled out of a sentence a group already wrote rather than handed down as a list.

\textbf{The sentence to land:} \emph{``Four things or nothing: shape, center, spread, unusual
features --- and every one of them in context. A picture that leans is named for where its tail
goes, not where its pile sits.''} Say it, write it, and point at the two leaning pictures while
you do.
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Application --- \emph{The Sleep Survey} (worked together, 6 min)]{sky}
The first place the four-part description is \emph{used}, on a fresh dotplot of the hours of
sleep 30 students reported on a school night. Part (a) is reading --- 30 students, and one lone
student at 4 hours. Part (b) names the shape and, more importantly, \textbf{says what the tail
means about these students} rather than about the picture. \textbf{Part (c) is the AP standard
enforced}: one paragraph, all four parts, in context. It is the first time all four are said
together.

\textbf{Students hold the pen, you hold the questions:} \emph{``Which side runs longer --- so
which way is it skewed?''} \quad \emph{``You said `left-skewed.' Say it again, but tell me about
students, not about the graph.''} \quad \emph{``Read me your paragraph and count the four on your
fingers as you go.''}

\emph{No \texttt{work} block in this lesson:} Topic 1.6 asks for description, not computation ---
the numbers arrive in Lesson 1.5. Page lockstep between the blank and the key rests entirely on
\texttt{\textbackslash answerspace}.
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[AP Practice --- AP-style practice (assigned, not scored)]{goldbox}
Four AP-style multiple-choice items on one commute-time histogram for 40 employees ---
right-skewed with a single employee stranded past 80 minutes --- then one multi-part free
response on the same display. \textbf{The distractors are the lesson:} MC~2 offers ``skewed
left, because most of the data lies on the left of the graph,'' which is the target
misconception in AP clothing, and MC~3 offers a conclusion about employees everywhere, which
EK UNC-1.H.7 forbids. \textbf{Parts (a) and (d) are the spiral} --- (a) reaches back to Lesson
1.1 (classify the variable before describing it) and (d) to Lesson 1.3 (check the interval width
before you believe the shape). Reusing one display for both sections is deliberate: the context
is already loaded, so the thinking stays on the description.

\textbf{Not scored --- the cover's score column reads \textbf{NA} for this page.} Go over it at
the start of Lesson~1.5 and hold the AP standard out loud: \emph{in context or it does not
count}. ``Skewed right with an outlier'' earns nothing; ``skewed right, so most employees have
short commutes while a few travel much longer, and one employee commutes over 80 minutes''
earns everything.
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Homework --- the graded practice, started in class]{redbox}
Five exercises spanning Topic 1.6: \textbf{1} match four displays to four shape descriptions;
\textbf{2} the misconception head-on --- a pile on the left with a tail to the right, and a
student who called it skewed left; \textbf{3} name the unusual feature in the bimodal display and
give a plausible reason two groups exist; \textbf{4} a full four-part description, in context, of
a cereal-sugar dotplot; \textbf{5} the AP-style multiple-choice item with a one-sentence
justification. Pairs for 1--3, solo for 4--5.

\textbf{This is the lesson's scored work} --- the cover's score column carries a blank for it and
it is collected next class. Students begin it in the last eight minutes of the period so that the
questions get asked while you are still in the room; they finish it at home.

\textbf{Eight minutes will not finish five items --- that is by design.} Point them at 1, 2,
and~5 in class, where you can catch a wrong start; 3 and~4 travel home cleanly.
\textbf{Item~2 is the diagnostic}: a student who cannot correct ``skewed left'' here will lose
the point every time this shape appears for the rest of the year. \textbf{Item~5 carries the
second check:} distractor (C) generalizes to every company in the industry, which is exactly
EK UNC-1.H.7. When you score the page, sort item~4 into piles by \emph{how many of the four}
appeared --- most students who lose credit lose it by writing three, not by writing a wrong one.
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Watch For (while circulating)]{redbox}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, nosep]
  \item \textbf{``It leans left, so it's skewed left''} (2a, HW~2, AP~2 --- the target
        misconception). Probe: \emph{``Which side runs out of data first? The name follows the
        long side.''}
  \item \textbf{The typical value taken as the whole answer} (1b, 1c, AP FRQ~c). Probe:
        \emph{``Both lines say 4 minutes. Are you telling me they are the same line?''}
  \item \textbf{Deleting the far-out value} (1d, AP~3). Students want to erase 13 minutes.
        Probe: \emph{``Is it wrong, or is it just inconvenient? What would make it wrong?''}
  \item \textbf{One ``center'' reported for a two-humped display} (2c, HW~3). Probe:
        \emph{``Point at the club member who is 67 inches tall.''}
  \item \textbf{Description with no context} (2d, Application~c, HW~4). ``Skewed right, center
        about 6, spread 2 to 21'' names no variable and no units. Probe: \emph{``Six what? Of
        what?''}
  \item \textbf{Over-claiming} (AP~3, HW~5, EK UNC-1.H.7): describing these 40 employees and
        concluding something about all employees. Probe: \emph{``Who exactly did we measure?''}
\end{itemize}
Cold-call prompts: \emph{``Give me a variable whose distribution should be skewed right, and say
why.''} \quad \emph{``Name a display where a single typical value would mislead somebody.''}
\end{skillbox}

% ── Teacher notes — one per component, in packet order ────────────────────────
\begin{teachernote}[Warm-Up]
Item~3 is the seed; item~2 is the drill; item~1 is a 30-second spiral that also protects AP
Practice item~4 --- do not let it expand. The whole warm-up is four minutes. \textbf{Capture item
3's sentence in a student's own words on the board and do not erase it.} You will point at it
twice in the debrief: once to name \emph{skewed}, once to name \emph{outlier}. \textbf{Do not say
\emph{shape}, \emph{skewed}, \emph{center}, \emph{spread}, \emph{outlier}, \emph{gap}, or
\emph{cluster} in this debrief.}
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Experience \& Formalize]
\textbf{Activity (20 min).} Roughly 8 minutes on problem~1, 12 on problem~2. Problem~1 looks
trivial and is not: the point of 1a is only to establish that \emph{both middles are 4}, so if a
group is slow there, give them the number and move them to 1b, which is the discovery. Do not
let any group leave 1c with ``it hides the spread'' --- push until they name a customer:
\emph{``it hides that somebody waited 13 minutes.''}

\textbf{Problem 2c is the one to protect the time for.} Groups will answer 2a and 2b quickly and
then get stuck on 2c, because ``a typical height that belongs to nobody'' is genuinely strange
the first time. Let them sit in it. If a group is truly stalled, ask only: \emph{``Point at the
club member who is 67 inches tall.''}

\textbf{The single discipline that makes this lesson work: do not name anything while
circulating.} A group that hears ``skewed right'' stops discovering, and the naming convention is
precisely what they were about to derive.

\textbf{Debrief (12 min).} Must-land: step~2 (skew is named for the tail) and step~5 (the
four-part description, assembled from their own 2d sentence). If time is short, compress step~4
--- gap and cluster are quick on a display they have already argued about --- but never compress
step~2.

\textbf{Application (6 min).} Part (c) is the AP standard. If you only get through (a) and (c),
that is the right trade.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[AP Practice]
\textbf{This page is not required and not scored} --- the graded homework is the section behind it
at the back of the packet. Work it as AP-format reps and go over it at the start of Lesson~1.5.
\textbf{MC item~2 is the one worth reading closely}: distractor (B) says ``skewed left, because
most of the commutes are on the left side of the graph,'' which is the exact reasoning the
activity was built to overturn. If more than a handful pick it, put the volleyball and home-price
pictures back up for three minutes before starting 1.5 --- this misconception does not fade on
its own, and it costs a point on every describe-the-distribution item for the rest of the year.
On the free response, part (c) is the highest-value part: it forces students to say what a single
average conceals, which is Skill 2.A in the form the exam actually asks it.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Homework]
\textbf{This is the graded page} --- collect it next class and score it. The eight minutes at the
end of the period are a \emph{start}, not a completion: put them on 1, 2, and~5 and let 3 and~4 go
home. Item~2 is the one to read first when scoring, and the correction must include the reason
(the tail, not the pile), not just the word ``right.'' Item~4 is the cheapest diagnostic in the
set: count how many of the four parts appear, and require the units --- ``center about 6'' is not
an answer; ``about 6 grams of sugar per serving'' is. Hold the context line while scoring, and on
item~5 accept only justifications that stay with \emph{these} 250 employees.
\end{teachernote}
