\boxguard[14]
\begin{objectivebox}
\small
By the end of these notes I will be able to:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em, nosep, after=\vspace{2pt}]
  \item name the \textbf{shape} of a distribution --- symmetric, skewed left, or skewed right;
        unimodal, bimodal, or uniform --- and say why the \emph{tail} decides the skew's name;
  \item point out the \textbf{unusual features} of a display --- outliers, gaps, and clusters ---
        and say why an outlier is investigated rather than deleted;
  \item describe a distribution completely --- \textbf{shape, unusual features, center, and
        variability} --- with every one of the four stated in context, naming the variable and
        its units.
\end{enumerate}
\end{objectivebox}

\vspace{6pt}

\boxguard[16]
\begin{vocabbox}
\small
Fill in each term \textbf{as we name it} in the notes below.
\par
\termans{Unimodal, bimodal, uniform}{one main peak; two clear peaks; all bars about equal, with no peak at all.}
\par
\termans{Symmetric}{the left half of the distribution is roughly a mirror image of the right half.}
\par
\termans{Outlier}{a value that sits unusually far from the rest of the data --- investigated, never simply deleted.}
\par
\termans{Gap and cluster}{a gap is a stretch where nothing was observed; a cluster is a bunch of values set off by a gap.}
\par
\termans{Skewed right, skewed left}{named for the side the longer tail runs out on --- right: a few values much larger; left: a few much smaller.}
\par
\end{vocabbox}

\boxguard[12]
\begin{notesbox}{1. One Number Is Never a Description --- and What Shape Looks Like}
\small
Last lesson you learned to \emph{read} these displays. Today you learn to \emph{say what they
show} --- and one number is never enough.

\vspace{5pt}
A supermarket timed 20 customers in each of its two checkout lines. Both plots use the same
scale, so you can compare them directly.

\vspace{3pt}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.80]

  \end{scope}

\end{center}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Read them.} Counting in from both ends, the middle wait in Line A is \ans{4}
minutes and the middle wait in Line B is \ans{4} minutes.

\vspace{5pt}
The \textbf{center} of a distribution is that middle, typical value --- where the data
\ans{pile} up. Both lines have the \ans{same} center. The \textbf{variability}, or
\textbf{spread}, is how far the values reach \ans{from each other}. In Line A every wait falls between
\ans{3} and \ans{6} minutes; in Line B they run from \ans{1} all the way to
\ans{13}. (Next lesson puts numbers on both; today we describe them by eye.)

\vspace{5pt}
\begin{center}
\fcolorbox{redacc}{redbg}{\parbox{0.94\linewidth}{\small
\textbf{Careful --- one number is not a description.} The manager reports
\emph{``the typical wait is 4 minutes in both lines.''} That sentence is \ans{true} of both
lines and tells a customer \ans{nothing} about which one to join. A center reported without the
\ans{spread} beside it is a summary of nothing.}}
\end{center}

\vspace{10pt}
\textbf{\textcolor{navy}{Shape: Count the Peaks, Then Check the Mirror.}}
Three different things were measured, each drawn as a histogram. \textbf{I} --- sale prices of
the 40 homes sold in one town last year. \textbf{II} --- scores of 32 students on a quiz most of
the class found easy. \textbf{III} --- heights of the 40 adults in a mixed volleyball club.

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Count the peaks first.} One main peak: \ans{unimodal}. Two clear peaks: \ans{bimodal}.
All the bars about equal, with no peak at all: \ans{uniform}. Pictures I and II each have
\ans{one} peak; Picture III has \ans{two}.

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Then check the mirror.} A distribution is \textbf{symmetric} when the left half is
roughly a \ans{mirror image} of the right. Picture \ans{III} is symmetric. Pictures I and II are
not: each one \emph{leans}, with its values piled at one end and a few stragglers trailing off
toward the other. Fill the table with what you see --- the name for that lean comes in
section~2, and it is not the name most people guess.

% ── I DO: section 2 — unusual features, the four-part description, and the crux ──
\boxguard[12]
\begin{notesbox}{2. Unusual Features, the Four-Part Description --- and the Tail That Names a Skew}
\small
Line B is not just wider than Line A --- two of its customers are somewhere else entirely.

\vspace{4pt}
An \textbf{outlier} is a value that sits unusually far \ans{from} the rest of the data. In
Line B the customers at \ans{11} and \ans{13} minutes are outliers. A \textbf{gap} is a
stretch of the number line where \ans{nothing} was observed --- here, everything from about 7
minutes to 11. A \textbf{cluster} is a group of values bunched together and set off from the
other values by a \ans{gap}. Line B has one big cluster from 1 to 7 minutes and two
stragglers; Picture III has \ans{two} clusters and nobody in between.

\vspace{6pt}
\begin{center}
\fcolorbox{navy}{sky}{\parbox{0.92\linewidth}{\centering\small
\textbf{Four things or nothing --- \ans{SOCS}}\\
\textbf{S}hape \quad$\cdot$\quad \textbf{O}utliers and other unusual features \quad$\cdot$\quad
\textbf{C}enter \quad$\cdot$\quad \textbf{S}pread (variability)\\
and every one of the four stated \ans{in context}, naming the variable and its units.}}
\end{center}

\vspace{5pt}
\textbf{In context} means a stranger could use the sentence. \emph{``Center about 4, spread 1
to 13''} is not a description of anything. \emph{``The typical wait in Line B was about 4
\ans{minutes}, with waits running from 1 to 13 minutes''} is. \textbf{One limit on what you may
claim:} describing these 20 customers is a statement about \ans{these} 20 customers. It is
not a claim about the store's lines in general; at most it \ans{suggests} something worth
investigating with data collected for that purpose.

\vspace{10pt}
\textbf{\textcolor{navy}{Skewed Which Way? The Tail Decides.}}
Look again at Picture I in section~1. Count the homes in the bars:

\begin{work}
\text{Homes that sold below \$400{,}000} &= 6 + 14 + 10 = 30 \text{ of the } 40 \\[4pt]
\text{Homes that sold above \$600{,}000} &= 1 + 1 = 2 \text{ of the } 40
\end{work}

Thirty of the forty homes sold for under \$400{,}000, and the picture still stretches out past
\$700{,}000 --- because two houses really did sell for that. The pile is on the \ans{left};
the long thin tail runs to the \ans{right}.

\vspace{4pt}
We call Picture I \textbf{skewed right}. Read that again: the data is piled on the \emph{left},
and the name says \emph{right}.

\vspace{5pt}
\begin{center}
\fcolorbox{navy}{sky}{\parbox{0.92\linewidth}{\centering\small
\textbf{The name of a skew follows the \ans{tail}, not the \ans{pile}.}\\
Skewed \textbf{right}: the longer tail runs right, so most values are \ans{small} and a few
are much larger. Skewed \textbf{left}: the longer tail runs \ans{left}.}}
\end{center}

\vspace{5pt}
Picture II is the mirror image. The quiz was easy, so most scores pile up on the
\ans{right}, and the few students who struggled trail off to the left. Picture II is skewed
\ans{left} --- and saying \emph{``skewed right, because the bars on the right are tallest''}
gets it exactly backwards.

\vspace{6pt}
\textbf{Symmetric does not mean ``nothing to report.''} Picture III is symmetric, and it is also
\ans{bimodal}, with a gap between about \ans{66} and \ans{70} inches. Somebody who
reports only \emph{``the typical height in this club is about 68 inches''} has said something
true of the picture and false of every \ans{adult} in it: the club is roughly half shorter
adults and half taller ones, and \ans{not one} of them is 68 inches tall. Shape is never one
word, and center is never the whole answer.

\vspace{6pt}
\textbf{And an outlier is a finding, not an error.} Go back to Line B's 13-minute customer.
Check her, do not \ans{delete} her. If the 13 is a typing error, fix the record; if she is a
real person with a full cart and a price check, she is precisely the customer the manager needs
to hear about --- and deleting her would hide the one thing worth investigating.

\vspace{6pt}
\textbf{Now say all four for Picture I.} \textbf{Shape:} the distribution of sale prices is
\ans{skewed right} and unimodal. \textbf{Unusual features:} no gaps; the tail thins out to
\ans{two} homes above \$600{,}000. \textbf{Center:} the middle home sold for about
\$\ans{300{,}000}. \textbf{Spread:} prices run from about \$100{,}000 to \$\ans{800{,}000}.
\end{notesbox}

\begin{practicebox}
\small
\textbf{The Sleep Survey --- we work this one together.} Thirty students reported how many hours
they slept on a school night.

\vspace{3pt}
\begin{center}

  \draw[->, thick] (-0.2,0) -- (8.2,0);

  \foreach \x/\n in {0.6/1, 3.0/1, 3.6/2, 4.2/4, 4.8/5, 5.4/7, 6.0/6, 6.6/3, 7.2/1} {
    \foreach \k in {1,...,\n} { \fill[navy] (\x,{0.2*\k}) circle (0.07); }}
  \node[font=\tiny] at (4.0,-0.85) {Hours of sleep on a school night};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\alph*.}, leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=7pt]
  \item How many students are shown? \ans{30} \quad How many peaks? \ans{one} \quad
        One value sits apart from all the others, at \ans{4} hours, behind a gap running
        from \ans{4 to 6} hours.
  \item Which way does this distribution lean? \ans{skewed left} \quad \textbf{Point at the tail},
        then say what that longer tail means \emph{for these students} --- not for the
        graph.\\[3pt]
        \ansline{The tail runs left: a few students got far less sleep than the rest, one all the way}
        \ansline{down at 4 hours. Most of these students slept 7 to 9 hours --- short nights are rare.}
  \item \textbf{Now say all four.} Write the complete description in one paragraph: shape,
        unusual features, center, and spread, in context, with the variable and its units
        named.\\[3pt]
        \ansline{Shape: the distribution of sleep hours is skewed left, with a single peak at 8 hours.}
        \ansline{Unusual: the student at 4 hours is an outlier, behind a gap from 4 to 6 hours.}
        \ansline{Center: a typical student in this group slept about 8 hours on a school night.}
        \ansline{Spread: the 30 values run from 4 to 9.5 hours; 29 of the 30 fall between 6 and 9.5.}
\end{enumerate}
\end{practicebox}
