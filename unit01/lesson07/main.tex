\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=sky, colframe=navy, boxrule=0.9pt, arc=2mm,
    left=2mm, right=2mm, top=1.5mm, bottom=1.5mm]
    \textbf{Primary Objective:} Students will distinguish a parameter from a statistic, compute
    and interpret a $z$-score in context, use the empirical rule to find approximate proportions
    under a normal model, and compare the relative positions of values drawn from two different
    distributions.
    \\[2pt]
    \textbf{CED alignment:} Topic 1.10 \quad$\cdot$\quad Big Idea: VAR (Variation and
    Distribution) \quad$\cdot$\quad Skills 2.D, 3.A \quad$\cdot$\quad LO VAR-2.A, VAR-2.B,
    VAR-2.C \quad$\cdot$\quad EK VAR-2.A.1--2.A.4, 2.B.1--2.B.4, 2.C.1
    \\[2pt]
    \textbf{Lesson model:} \emph{Experience first, formalize later.} Students work the activity
    in small groups using only prior knowledge; the teacher circulates with questions, cues, and
    prompts (not answers); the debrief attaches the formal vocabulary to what the groups already
    found. \textbf{No vocabulary before the debrief.}
    \\[2pt]
    \textbf{Note on the ``no sketch'' rule:} every dotplot, histogram, and normal curve in this
    lesson is \textbf{given}. Students read, annotate, and compute from them. Do not add a ``draw
    the normal curve'' step --- the exam asks students to \emph{use} the model, and hand-drawing
    a bell shape teaches nothing about $\mu$, $\sigma$, or the empirical rule.
\end{tcolorbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Learning Targets \& Key Understandings]{goldbox}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X|X@{}}
\textbf{Learning targets (student language)} & \textbf{Key understandings (the ``why'')} \\[2pt]
\hline
\vspace{-6pt}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, nosep, after=\vspace{2pt}]
  \item I can say why the bigger raw number is not always the better result.
  \item I can measure how far a value sits from its own group's average, counted in that
        group's own typical spread.
  \item I can use that measurement to compare results from two completely different settings.
  \item I can read the same three percentages off any mound-shaped pile of data.
\end{itemize}
&
\vspace{-6pt}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, nosep, after=\vspace{2pt}]
  \item A \textbf{parameter} summarizes a population ($\mu$, $\sigma$); a \textbf{statistic}
        summarizes a sample ($\bar x$, $s_x$). Same idea, different object (EK VAR-2.A.1).
  \item A normal curve is mound-shaped and symmetric and is fixed entirely by $\mu$ and $\sigma$
        (EK VAR-2.A.2, 2.A.4).
  \item $z = (x_i - \mu)/\sigma$ counts standard deviations, which is a \emph{unitless} count ---
        that is exactly why it can compare grams to seconds (EK VAR-2.B.1, 2.B.2, 2.C.1).
  \item \textbf{Target misconception:} that the higher raw score is the better performance.
        Students reach for the bigger number in 1a and then stall, because 12.0 metres and
        12.3 seconds are not on the same scale. \textbf{That stall is the lesson.}
  \item \textbf{Second misconception:} that 68--95--99.7 applies to any data set. It applies to a
        \emph{mound-shaped, symmetric} one; a skewed display voids it (EK VAR-2.A.3).
\end{itemize}
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[{Vocabulary, Concepts, \& Theorems --- attached in the debrief, not before}]{greenbox}
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.48\linewidth}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Parameter} --- a numerical summary of a \emph{population} (EK VAR-2.A.1)
        \item \textbf{$\mu$, $\sigma$} --- the population mean and population standard
              deviation; contrast with $\bar x$ and $s_x$ from Lesson 1.5
        \item \textbf{Normal distribution / normal curve} --- mound-shaped and symmetric, fixed
              by $\mu$ and $\sigma$ (EK VAR-2.A.2)
        \item \textbf{Approximately normal} --- the honest phrase; real data is never exactly
              normal (EK VAR-2.A.4)
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.48\linewidth}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Empirical rule (68--95--99.7)} --- approximate proportions within 1, 2, and
              3 standard deviations of $\mu$ (EK VAR-2.A.3)
        \item \textbf{Standardized score / $z$-score} --- $z = (x_i - \mu)/\sigma$
              (EK VAR-2.B.1, 2.B.2)
        \item \textbf{Relative position} --- $z$-scores and percentiles compare points within or
              between data sets (EK VAR-2.C.1)
        \item \textbf{Carried forward} --- mean and standard deviation (1.5); percentile and the
              five-number summary (1.6); shape, centre, spread (1.4)
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Warm-Up --- Activate Prior Knowledge]{sky}
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.48\linewidth}
    \textbf{The three items and what each seeds}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{1.} Two five-number summaries, two IQRs. \textbf{Spirals} Lesson 1.6 ---
              and quietly re-establishes that ``more spread out'' is a measurable thing.
        \item \textbf{2.} A mean of 20 and a standard deviation of 3; how many 3-point steps
              from the mean is 26? is 17? \textbf{Seeds:} the exact arithmetic 1c asks for,
              done once with numbers that divide evenly.
        \item \textbf{3.} 360 of 400 inside a range; what percent is outside, and what percent
              past the high end? \textbf{Seeds:} the halving-the-tail move that 2d and the
              Application both need.
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.48\linewidth}
    \textbf{Running it}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Name nothing.} Do not say \emph{z-score}, \emph{standardized},
              \emph{normal}, \emph{parameter}, or \emph{empirical rule}. Item~2 is arithmetic
              on prior knowledge, and that is all it is today.
        \item Item~2 uses numbers that divide evenly on purpose. If a student asks ``what does
              2 steps \emph{mean}?'', say ``hold that,'' write the question on the board, and
              point at it in the debrief.
        \item Debrief item~3 aloud and leave \emph{``symmetric, so split the leftovers evenly''}
              on the board for the whole period. Problem 2d and Application (b) both live on it.
        \item The percentages in item~3 are 90 / 10 / 5 \textbf{on purpose} --- not 95 and 2.5.
              Using the real numbers here would hand away the discovery in Problem~2.
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Experience \& Formalize --- The Activity: \emph{Two Tryouts}]{sky}
\textbf{Launch (2 min).} \emph{``One scholarship, two athletes, two different events. Maya threw
12.0 metres. Dev ran 12.3 seconds. Decide who had the better day --- and be ready to defend it to
the athlete who loses.''} Groups of 3--4. Do not preview any vocabulary, and do not rescue 1a.

\begin{multicols}{2}
\textbf{What students do.} \emph{Problem 1} runs the misconception on purpose. \textbf{1a} asks
whose number is bigger and whether that settles it --- almost every group says Dev, then reads
the units and stalls. \textbf{1b} gets them to subtract each result from its own event's average:
$+3.0$ metres and $-0.9$ seconds. Better --- and still not comparable. \textbf{1c is the crux}:
divide each difference by that event's own typical spread. Maya is 2.0 spreads above her event's
average; Dev is 1.5 spreads below his, and \emph{below is better} in a sprint. \textbf{1d}
introduces an athlete sitting exactly at the average (the answer is 0) and asks what a negative
value means.

\emph{Problem 2} is the second discovery. Two given frequency tables --- 500 bread loaves and 400
body temperatures --- already sorted into bands. \textbf{2a} is arithmetic: $341/500$, $476/500$,
$499/500$. \textbf{2b} asks for a prediction \emph{before} computing the temperature percentages,
then computes them: $273/400$, $381/400$, $399/400$. \textbf{2c} asks what two unrelated data sets
could possibly share that produces the same three numbers, and what would break it. \textbf{2d} is
the halving move from warm-up~3.

\columnbreak
\textbf{What the teacher does.} Circulate. Listen before speaking. When a group stalls, give a
\emph{question, cue, or prompt}, not an answer:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, nosep]
  \item \textbf{1a:} ``Write the units next to each number. Now read your sentence out loud.''
  \item \textbf{1b:} ``You have $+3.0$ and $-0.9$. Three what? Nine-tenths of what?''
  \item \textbf{1c (the crux):} ``The table gives you the typical spread for each event. How many
        of \emph{Maya's} typical spreads is 3.0 metres? How many of \emph{Dev's} is 0.9 seconds?''
        Then: ``In the sprint, which direction is the good direction?''
  \item \textbf{1d:} ``Do the same arithmetic with a difference of zero. What comes out?''
  \item \textbf{2b:} ``Commit to a prediction in pen before you touch the second table.''
  \item \textbf{2c:} ``Cover the axis labels on both pictures. Now what is different about them?''
\end{itemize}
While circulating, pick the work to display: one group's 1a stall, one group's 1c comparison, and
one group's written prediction from 2b.
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Debrief --- Formalize (what goes on the board, in a second color)]{sky}
Work through the displayed student work in order and \textbf{attach each formal name to
something a group already wrote}. Students fill their QuickNotes line by line as you go.
\begin{multicols}{2}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.3em, nosep, itemsep=3pt]
  \item Problem 2's tables described \emph{every} loaf and \emph{every} reading $\to$
        \emph{parameter} (EK VAR-2.A.1), and the notation contrast: \textbf{$\mu$ and $\sigma$
        describe a population; $\bar x$ and $s_x$ from Lesson 1.5 describe a sample.} Write all
        four symbols on the board in two columns and leave them up --- this contrast pays off in
        every remaining unit of the course.
  \item The two mound-shaped histograms in Problem 2 $\to$ \emph{normal distribution} /
        \emph{normal curve}: mound-shaped and symmetric, fixed entirely by $\mu$ and $\sigma$
        (EK VAR-2.A.2). Say \emph{approximately} normal every time (EK VAR-2.A.4).
  \item Their 2a and 2b percentages $\to$ the \emph{empirical rule}, 68--95--99.7
        (EK VAR-2.A.3). Write their own numbers first --- 68.2, 95.2, 99.8 and 68.25, 95.25,
        99.75 --- \emph{then} round them into the rule. The rule must arrive as a summary of
        their arithmetic, not as an announcement.
  \item Their 1c division $\to$ \emph{standardized score}, then \emph{$z$-score}, with the formula
        $z = (x_i - \mu)/\sigma$ written directly beside their own $3.0 \div 1.5$
        (EK VAR-2.B.1, 2.B.2). Then 1d: $z = 0$ at the mean, $z < 0$ below it.
  \item 1c as a whole $\to$ \emph{comparing relative position} (EK VAR-2.C.1): $z$-scores and
        percentiles put two different data sets on the same footing. Name percentile here so
        Lesson 1.6 gets tied in.
  \item One closing line only (EK VAR-2.B.3, 2.B.4): technology --- a calculator or a standard
        normal table --- converts a $z$-score into an exact proportion. \textbf{We do not do that
        today.} It returns in Unit~5.
\end{enumerate}
\columnbreak
\textbf{QuickNotes.} Half a page, filled together. \textbf{It is a summary of what they found,
not a new lecture}; if you are talking for more than 90 seconds without pointing at student
work, stop.

\textbf{Why this order.} Parameter first, because $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are the symbols every other
line uses. The curve second, because the empirical rule is a fact \emph{about that shape} and
students must see the shape requirement attached before they hear the percentages. The
percentages third, from their own arithmetic. The $z$-score fourth --- it is the more powerful
idea, but it lands harder when the room has already accepted that a distribution's own spread is
the natural ruler. Comparison last, because it is what the whole activity was for.

\textbf{The sentence to land:} \emph{Points, seconds, grams, and degrees cannot be compared.
Standard deviations can. Once you count in standard deviations, every distribution speaks the
same language --- and if it is mound-shaped, it always says 68, 95, and 99.7.} Say it, write it,
and point at Maya's 2.0 and Dev's $-1.5$ while you do.
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Application --- \emph{The Bread Loaf} (worked together, 6 min)]{sky}
Deliberately the \emph{same} bakery as Problem 2, so no new context has to be loaded in six
minutes. $\mu = 800$ g, $\sigma = 20$ g. Part (a) is a $z$-score in a \texttt{work} block plus the
one-sentence interpretation the exam scores. \textbf{Part (b) is the empirical rule alone}: 840 g
is exactly $\mu + 2\sigma$, so $(100 - 95)/2 \approx 2.5\%$ --- warm-up~3 in the real numbers.
\textbf{Part (c) is the judgment part}, and it is the one to protect time for: a customer calls a
754 g loaf ``way underweight,'' $z = -2.3$, and the honest answer is that the complaint has merit
\emph{and} that the normal model is only approximate.

\textbf{Students hold the pen, you hold the questions:} \emph{``What are the units on 1.7?''}
\quad \emph{``840 is how many spreads above 800? So what does the rule already tell you?''}
\quad \emph{``Is $-2.3$ unusual, or just below average? Which word does the evidence support?''}

\emph{Two \texttt{work} blocks in this lesson's Application:} both are authored byte-identically
in the blank and the key, so pagination cannot drift. Do not edit one without the other.
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[AP Practice --- AP-style practice (assigned, not scored)]{goldbox}
Four AP-style multiple-choice items on newborn birth weights ($\mu = 3{,}300$ g,
$\sigma = 500$ g, approximately normal, with the curve given and its band boundaries marked) ---
a straight $z$-score computation, an empirical-rule proportion that requires \emph{halving} a
tail, a comparison across two different distributions (the lesson's crux in AP clothing), and
\textbf{one item whose correct answer is that the empirical rule does not apply}, because the
given length-of-stay histogram is strongly right-skewed. That last one is a real AP item type and
the honest limit of the model. Then one multi-part free response on the same birth-weight record:
compute and interpret a $z$-score, use the empirical rule, compare relative position against a
second hospital's distribution, and \textbf{part (d) spirals to Lessons 1.4 and 1.6} --- judge
from a display whether a normal model is reasonable at all, naming the shape features that decide
it.

\textbf{Not scored --- the cover's score column reads \textbf{NA} for this page.} Go over it in
the unit-test review and hold the AP standard out loud: \emph{in context or it does not count}.
``$z = -1.5$'' earns nothing on its own; ``this newborn weighs 1.5 standard deviations less than
the mean birth weight of 3,300 g'' is the answer.
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Homework --- the graded practice, started in class]{redbox}
Five exercises spanning Topic 1.10: \textbf{1} parameter or statistic, on four given summaries;
\textbf{2} three $z$-scores from one distribution, including $z = 0$ and a negative one, with the
negative one interpreted in a sentence; \textbf{3} the crux head-on --- two students, two tests,
different means and standard deviations, higher raw score loses; \textbf{4} the empirical rule off
a given normal curve, including a tail proportion; \textbf{5} the AP-style multiple-choice item
with a one-sentence justification. Pairs for 1--3, solo for 4--5.

\textbf{This is the lesson's scored work} --- the cover's score column carries a blank for it and
it is collected next class. Students begin it in the last eight minutes of the period so that the
questions get asked while you are still in the room; they finish it at home.

\textbf{Eight minutes will not finish five items --- that is by design.} Point them at 1, 2,
and~3 in class, where a wrong start is cheap to catch; 4 and~5 travel home cleanly.
\textbf{Item~3 is where the misconception shows}: a student who answers ``Ben, he scored 88'' has
not made the turn this lesson exists to make. \textbf{Item~5 carries the check:} it is item~3 with
the direction reversed, in a race where a \emph{lower} time is better, and distractor (C) reaches
the right athlete by the wrong reasoning. When you score the page, sort item~5 into three piles ---
chose (B) and justified it with both $z$-scores (ready); chose (B) but justified it with the raw
times (ready, needs the language); chose (A), (C), or (E) (the misconception survived). If more
than a third land in the last pile, reopen Maya and Dev in the unit-test review.
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Watch For (while circulating)]{redbox}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, nosep]
  \item \textbf{``Dev, because 12.3 is bigger''} (1a --- the target misconception). Do not
        correct it; make it collide with the units. Probe: \emph{``Bigger what? Write the unit
        after each number and read it back to me.''}
  \item \textbf{Stopping at the difference} (1b, HW~3): $+3.0$ versus $-0.9$ feels decisive.
        Probe: \emph{``Is 3.0 metres a lot in the shot put? Look at the dotplot before you
        answer.''}
  \item \textbf{Sign thrown away} (1c, 1d, HW~2, HW~5, AP~3): students report Dev as ``1.5'' and
        lose the whole sprint insight. Probe: \emph{``Faster or slower than average? So which
        side of the mean?''}
  \item \textbf{Lower-is-better inverted} (1c, HW~5): having learned that negative means below,
        students conclude Dev did badly. Probe: \emph{``Would you rather be above or below
        average in a 100-metre time?''}
  \item \textbf{68--95--99.7 applied to anything} (2c, AP~4): the second misconception, and the
        one the exam tests. Probe: \emph{``What did the two pictures in Problem~2 have in common?
        Does this one have it?''}
  \item \textbf{The whole tail instead of half} (2d, Application b, HW~4c, AP~2): students answer
        5\% where 2.5\% is wanted. Probe: \emph{``Five percent is outside on \emph{both} ends.
        How much is on the end you were asked about?''}
  \item \textbf{$\mu$ and $\bar x$ used interchangeably} (HW~1, debrief step 1). Probe:
        \emph{``Did somebody measure every loaf, or a handful of loaves?''}
\end{itemize}
Cold-call prompts: \emph{``Give me a value with a $z$-score of zero.''} \quad \emph{``Name a
variable where a negative $z$-score is good news.''}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{teachernote}[Warm-Up]
Four minutes, five at the outside. Item~1 is a 45-second spiral --- do not let the boxplot
conversation reopen. Item~2 is mechanical and exists so that dividing by the spread is not the
obstacle inside 1c; if the room is quick, take 60 seconds and move on. \textbf{Item~3 is the one
to debrief aloud}, and the sentence you want on the board in the students' own words is
\emph{``it is even on both sides, so split the leftover in half.''} Leave it there all period ---
Problem 2d, the Application, the homework, and the AP practice all reach back for it.
\textbf{Do not say \emph{z-score}, \emph{standardized}, \emph{normal}, \emph{parameter}, or
\emph{empirical rule} in this debrief.}
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Experience \& Formalize]
\textbf{Activity (20 min).} Roughly 10 minutes on problem~1, 10 on problem~2. \textbf{Protect the
stall in 1a.} A group that is confused at 1a is exactly on schedule; a teacher who resolves it
has spent the lesson's only real leverage. Let them write ``Dev,'' let them read the units, and
let them cross it out themselves.

\textbf{Make every group finish 1b before they reach 1c.} The two differences, $+3.0$ metres and
$-0.9$ seconds, are the step that proves subtraction is not enough --- groups that skip straight
to 1c divide without understanding why division is the fix.

\textbf{Problem 2b's prediction must be written before the second table is touched.} The moment
matters more than the arithmetic: a student who predicted ``totally different'' and then computed
68.25\% has had the experience this lesson is built on. Circulate specifically to enforce that
order.

\textbf{The single discipline that makes this lesson work: do not name anything while
circulating.} A group that hears ``z-score'' stops discovering.

\textbf{Debrief (12 min).} Must-land: the $\mu$/$\sigma$ versus $\bar x$/$s_x$ contrast (step 1),
the empirical rule built from their own six percentages (step 3), and the $z$-score formula
written beside their own $3.0 \div 1.5$ (step 4). If time is short, cut step 6 to one sentence ---
technology returns in Unit~5 and needs nothing today.

\textbf{Application (6 min).} Part (c) is the judgment part; if you only get through (a) and (c),
that is the right trade.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[AP Practice]
\textbf{This page is not required and not scored} --- the graded homework is the section behind it
at the back of the packet. Work it as AP-format reps and go over it in the unit-test review.
\textbf{MC item~4 is the one worth reading closely}: its correct answer is that the empirical rule
\emph{does not apply}, because the length-of-stay histogram is strongly right-skewed. Students who
have just spent a period being handed 68--95--99.7 will apply it to anything with an axis, and the
exam knows that. If more than a handful miss it, put the two Problem~2 histograms back on the
screen beside the skewed one and ask what is missing. On the free response, part (d) is the
highest-value part --- it asks whether the model is appropriate at all, which is Skill 2.D in the
form the exam actually asks it, and it is the part students most often skip.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Homework]
\textbf{This is the graded page} --- collect it next class and score it. The eight minutes at the
end of the period are a \emph{start}, not a completion: put them on 1, 2, and~3 and let 4 and~5 go
home. Item~3 is the one to read first when scoring; ``Ben, he scored 88'' means the lesson did not
land for that student, and it is worth two minutes with them individually before the unit test.
Item~2's third value is the diagnostic: a student who writes ``1.5'' instead of ``$-1.5$'' has
dropped the sign, and the sign is half the idea. Hold the context line while scoring --- an
interpretation that says ``$z = -1.5$'' without naming heart rate, the mean of 74, and the
direction is incomplete, exactly as it would be on the exam. \textbf{Because this is the last
content lesson of the unit}, the homework's preview box points at the unit test and Unit~2; say
the test date out loud when you assign it, since the box alone will not do it.
\end{teachernote}
