\boxguard[14]
\begin{objectivebox}
\small
By the end of these notes I will be able to:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em, nosep, after=\vspace{2pt}]
  \item classify a variable as \textbf{categorical} or \textbf{quantitative} using the amount
        test, not the ``does it have digits'' test;
  \item explain why averaging a ZIP code or a permit number is meaningless;
  \item explain what changes --- and what is lost --- when measured amounts are replaced by
        group labels.
\end{enumerate}
\end{objectivebox}

\vspace{0.10in}

\boxguard[16]
\begin{vocabbox}
\small
Fill in each term \textbf{as we name it} in the notes below.
\par
\termans{Categorical variable}{takes values that}{are category names or group labels --- Gold, Cedar Falls, Yes.}
\par
\termans{Quantitative variable}{takes numerical}{values for a measured or counted amount --- minutes, pounds, bottles.}
\par
\termans{The amount test}{ask whether the value}{records how much or how many, not whether it is written in digits.}
\par
\termans{Grouping (binning)}{replacing measured}{amounts with group labels; turns Q into C, and the mean cannot be recovered.}
\par
\end{vocabbox}

% ── I DO: section 1 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\boxguard[24]
\begin{notesbox}{1. Every Variable Is One of Two Kinds}
\small
Last lesson a \textbf{variable} was any characteristic recorded for each individual. Today we
split them in two --- and this single decision determines every graph and every summary you are
allowed to use for the rest of the course.

\vspace{6pt}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} >{\bfseries\color{navy}}p{0.28\linewidth} X @{}}
\hline
Categorical & Values are category \ans{names} or group labels. \\
Quantitative & Values are \ans{numerical} for a measured or counted amount. \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}

\vspace{8pt}
The same five hikers, two different kinds of column:

\vspace{4pt}
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
\begin{tabular}{@{} l l c @{}}
\rowcolor{sky}
\textbf{Party} & \textbf{Trail} & \textbf{Time out (min)} \\
\hline
Alvarez & Ridge Loop   &  95 \\
Boone   & Cedar Falls  & 140 \\
Delgado & Summit Trail & 215 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\vspace{4pt}
\emph{Trail} is \ans{categorical}: its values name which trail, and there is no sense in which
Summit Trail is ``more'' than Ridge Loop. \emph{Time out} is \ans{quantitative}: 215 minutes
really is more than 95, and the difference, 120 minutes, means something.
\end{notesbox}

\vspace{0.10in}

% ── I DO: section 2 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\boxguard[16]
\begin{notesbox}{2. The Amount Test}
\small
Do not ask ``does it have digits.'' Ask:

\vspace{6pt}
\begin{center}
\fcolorbox{navy}{sky}{\parbox{0.92\linewidth}{\centering\small
\textbf{Is the value an \emph{amount} of something --- how much, or how many?}\\[3pt]
If yes, the variable is \ans{quantitative}. If the value only \emph{identifies} or
\emph{labels}, it is \ans{categorical}.}}
\end{center}

\vspace{6pt}
A second way to run the same test: \textbf{does adding or averaging the values produce something
real?} If the average is meaningful, the variable is quantitative. If the average is
\ans{nonsense}, it is categorical no matter how it is written.
\end{notesbox}

\vspace{0.10in}

% ── I DO: section 3 — the trap ───────────────────────────────────────────────
\boxguard[26]
\begin{notesbox}{3. Written in Digits, and Still Categorical}
\small
Two hiking parties: permit numbers 2041 and 6318, carrying 6 and 2 water bottles. Both columns
are written entirely in digits. Both averages compute without complaint.

\begin{work}
\text{average permit \#} &= \frac{2041 + 6318}{2} = 4179.5 \\[4pt]
\text{average water bottles} &= \frac{6 + 2}{2} = 4
\end{work}

Four water bottles is a real amount --- it is what each party would have carried if the load were
shared evenly. Permit 4179.5 is \ans{meaningless}: it does not identify a party, and no party
carries ``half a permit.'' The digits in a permit number are a \ans{label}, not a
measurement.

\vspace{6pt}
\textbf{Classify each. Write C or Q.}

\vspace{4pt}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} Y Y Y Y Y @{}}
\textbf{Permit \#} & \textbf{Parking spot} & \textbf{Home ZIP} & \textbf{Water bottles} &
\textbf{Group size} \\
\ans{C} & \ans{C} & \ans{C} & \ans{Q} & \ans{Q} \\
\end{tabularx}

\vspace{6pt}
\textbf{Counting a categorical variable produces numbers.} ``62\% of parties hiked with a dog''
is a numerical \ans{summary} of a categorical variable. The \emph{summary} is numeric; the
\emph{variable} is still \ans{categorical}.
\end{notesbox}

\vspace{0.10in}

% ── I DO: section 4 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\boxguard[16]
\begin{notesbox}{4. Grouping Turns Q into C --- and Loses Something}
\small
A ranger stops recording \emph{Time out} in minutes and records \emph{Short} / \emph{Medium} /
\emph{Long} instead. Same hikes, same afternoon.

\vspace{6pt}
The variable is now \ans{categorical}. This is called \textbf{grouping} or
\textbf{binning}.

\vspace{6pt}
\textbf{What is lost:} once 95, 140, and 215 become labels, the mean time on the trail can no
longer be \ans{computed} from the column. Grouping runs \ans{one} way only ---
you can always bin amounts into labels, but you can never recover the amounts from the labels.
\end{notesbox}

\vspace{0.10in}

% ── WE DO: guided practice ───────────────────────────────────────────────────
\boxguard[20]
\begin{practicebox}
\small
\textbf{The Season Pass --- we work this one together.} A ski area records five things about each
of its 3{,}400 season-pass holders: \textbf{pass number}, \textbf{age} (years), \textbf{pass
tier} (Gold / Silver / Bronze), \textbf{miles traveled} to the mountain, and \textbf{locker
number}.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\alph*.}, leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=7pt]
  \item The individuals are \ans{the 3,400 pass holders}. Label each variable \textbf{C} or \textbf{Q}.

        \vspace{3pt}
        \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
        \begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} Y Y Y Y Y @{}}
        \textbf{Pass \#} & \textbf{Age} & \textbf{Pass tier} & \textbf{Miles traveled} &
        \textbf{Locker \#} \\
        \ans{C} & \ans{Q} & \ans{C} & \ans{Q} & \ans{C} \\
        \end{tabularx}
  \item The resort stops recording age in years and records \textbf{age group} instead
        (Under 18 / 18--34 / 35--54 / 55+). Same people, same afternoon --- which kind is the
        variable now, and what can the resort no longer compute?\\[3pt]
        \ansline{Categorical --- the values are now group labels rather than measured amounts.}
        \ansline{The resort can no longer compute the mean age of its pass holders.}
  \item A report states: \textbf{``The average pass number is 5{,}120.''} Say in one sentence
        why that number tells you nothing about the pass holders.\\[3pt]
        \ansline{A pass number identifies a person; it does not measure anything about them, so}
        \ansline{its average is not an amount of anything --- nobody is ``average'' at 5,120.}
\end{enumerate}
\end{practicebox}

\vspace{0.10in}

% ── YOU DO: independent practice ─────────────────────────────────────────────
\boxguard[20]
\begin{notesbox}{Independent Practice --- You Try}
\small
Work these on your own. I will circulate.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=8pt]
  \item A coffee shop logs every order it fills. Label each variable \textbf{C} or \textbf{Q}.

        \vspace{3pt}
        \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
        \begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} Y Y Y Y @{}}
        \textbf{Drink size (S/M/L)} & \textbf{Number of shots} & \textbf{Price paid (\$)} &
        \textbf{Loyalty card \#} \\
        \ans{C} & \ans{Q} & \ans{Q} & \ans{C} \\
        \end{tabularx}
  \item Name one variable written entirely in digits that is \textbf{not} quantitative, and say
        what its digits are doing instead of measuring.\\[3pt]
        \ansline{Answers vary: ZIP code, jersey number, bus route, cafeteria PIN, room number.}
        \ansline{The digits identify or label the individual rather than record an amount.}
  \item A survey records \emph{Number of pets owned} and \emph{Type of pet owned}. Classify each,
        then say what a summary of each column would look like.\\[3pt]
        \ansline{\emph{Number of pets} is quantitative --- summarize with a mean, such as 1.8 pets.}
        \ansline{\emph{Type of pet} is categorical --- summarize with counts or percents by type.}
\end{enumerate}
\end{notesbox}

\vspace{0.10in}

% ── YOU DO: the transfer set — a data set students have not seen ─────────────
\boxguard[30]
\begin{notesbox}{Putting It Together --- The Trailhead Register}
\small
Every party that hikes at Ross Hollow State Park signs the register at the trailhead. Last season
the ranger added four new columns --- \textbf{all four are written entirely in digits.} Still on
your own. \textbf{Every classification needs a reason.}

\vspace{5pt}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} l Y Y Y Y Y @{}}
\rowcolor{sky}
\textbf{Party} & \textbf{Permit \#} & \textbf{Parking spot} & \textbf{Home ZIP} &
\textbf{Water bottles} & \textbf{Dog along} \\
\hline
Alvarez & 2041 & 17 & 80211 & 6 & Yes \\
Boone   & 6318 & 04 & 80304 & 2 & No  \\
Delgado & 1150 & 11 & 80211 & 3 & No  \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}

\vspace{7pt}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\alph*.}, leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=6pt]
  \item Classify all five recorded variables. Write \textbf{C} or \textbf{Q}.

        \vspace{3pt}
        \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
        \begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} Y Y Y Y Y @{}}
        \textbf{Permit \#} & \textbf{Parking spot} & \textbf{Home ZIP} & \textbf{Water bottles} &
        \textbf{Dog along} \\
        \ans{C} & \ans{C} & \ans{C} & \ans{Q} & \ans{C} \\
        \end{tabularx}
  \item \textbf{Two of the digit columns are the ones people get wrong.} Name them, and say in
        one sentence each what those digits are doing instead of measuring.
        \answerspace{1.7cm}{\emph{Permit \#} and \emph{Home ZIP} (and \emph{Parking spot}).
        A permit number identifies which party pulled it --- the average, 4179.5, is nobody's
        permit. A ZIP code names a postal area; 80211 is not ``more'' than 80304.}
  \item The season report says \textbf{``62\% of parties hiked with a dog.''} That number came
        out of the \emph{Dog along} column. Does producing a percent make \emph{Dog along}
        quantitative? Explain.
        \answerspace{1.5cm}{No. Counting a categorical variable \emph{produces} numbers --- that
        is how categorical variables are summarized. The 62\% is a numerical summary; the values
        in the column are still Yes and No.}
  \item Next season the ranger stops recording \emph{Time out} in minutes and records
        \emph{Short}, \emph{Medium}, or \emph{Long} instead. Name the kind of variable it
        becomes, and one thing the ranger can no longer compute.
        \answerspace{1.5cm}{It becomes \textbf{categorical} --- the values are now group labels.
        The ranger can no longer compute the mean time on the trail, because ``Medium'' does not
        carry the 140 minutes it came from. Grouping runs one way only.}
\end{enumerate}
\end{notesbox}

% ── DEBRIEF (8 min, whole class — filled in together) ────────────────────────
\boxguard[18]
\begin{reflectionbox}
\small
\textbf{Debrief --- we fill this in together.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=6pt]
  \item State the amount test in one sentence, without using the word ``digits.''
        \answerspace{1.1cm}{Ask whether the value records an amount --- how much or how many.
        If it only identifies or labels the individual, the variable is categorical.}
  \item Give one variable that is quantitative and one that is categorical \emph{that were not
        on this register}, and say how you know.
        \answerspace{1.3cm}{Answers vary: \emph{height in inches} is Q --- 68 inches is an amount.
        \emph{Eye color} is C --- brown names a category and has no total.}
  \item Why can grouping never be undone?
        \answerspace{1.1cm}{Because the label keeps only which bin the value fell into, not the
        value. ``Long'' cannot be turned back into 215 minutes.}
\end{enumerate}
\end{reflectionbox}
