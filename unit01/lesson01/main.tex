\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=sky, colframe=navy, boxrule=0.9pt, arc=2mm,
    left=2mm, right=2mm, top=1.5mm, bottom=1.5mm]
    \textbf{Primary Objective:} Students will identify the variables recorded in a data set and
    classify each as categorical or quantitative --- judging by whether the values record a
    measured or counted amount rather than by whether they are written in digits.
    \\[2pt]
    \textbf{CED alignment:} Topic 1.2 \quad$\cdot$\quad Big Idea: VAR (Variation \& Distribution)
    \quad$\cdot$\quad Skill 2.A \quad$\cdot$\quad LO VAR-1.B, VAR-1.C \quad$\cdot$\quad
    EK VAR-1.B.1, VAR-1.C.1, VAR-1.C.2
    \\[2pt]
    \textbf{Lesson model:} \emph{Experience first, formalize later.} Students work the activity
    in small groups using only prior knowledge; the teacher circulates with questions, cues, and
    prompts (not answers); the debrief attaches the formal vocabulary to what the groups already
    found; the application and practice apply it to new contexts. No separate direct-instruction
    block, and \textbf{no vocabulary before the debrief.}
\end{tcolorbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Learning Targets \& Key Understandings]{goldbox}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X|X@{}}
\textbf{Learning targets (student language)} & \textbf{Key understandings (the ``why'')} \\[2pt]
\hline
\vspace{-6pt}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, nosep, after=\vspace{2pt}]
  \item I can sort the columns of a data set into two kinds that behave differently.
  \item I can explain why averaging one column is useful and averaging another is nonsense.
  \item I can say what a column really records even when every entry is written in digits.
  \item I can say what happens to a variable when its amounts are replaced by group names.
\end{itemize}
&
\vspace{-6pt}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, nosep, after=\vspace{2pt}]
  \item A variable is a characteristic that changes from one individual to another
        (EK VAR-1.B.1). Categorical values are category names or group labels
        (EK VAR-1.C.1); quantitative values are numerical, for a measured or counted quantity
        (EK VAR-1.C.2).
  \item \textbf{Target misconception:} that a column written in digits is quantitative.
        \emph{Permit \#}, \emph{Home ZIP}, and \emph{Parking spot} are all digits and all
        categorical. The discriminator is whether the value records an \emph{amount}, which is
        exactly why arithmetic on it is or is not meaningful.
  \item \textbf{Second misconception (the reverse trap):} that a variable summarized by a
        number is quantitative. Counting a categorical column \emph{always} yields counts and
        percents; the summary is numeric, the variable is not.
\end{itemize}
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Variable} --- a characteristic that changes from one individual to
              another (EK VAR-1.B.1)
        \item \textbf{Categorical variable} --- takes on values that are category names or
              group labels (EK VAR-1.C.1)
        \item \textbf{Quantitative variable} --- takes on numerical values for a measured or
              counted quantity (EK VAR-1.C.2)
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.48\linewidth}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{The amount test} --- ask whether the value records how much or how many,
              not whether it is written in digits
        \item \textbf{Grouping (binning)} --- replacing measured amounts with group labels;
              turns a quantitative variable into a categorical one, and the mean cannot be
              recovered
        \item \textbf{Carried forward from Lesson 1.0} --- individual, value, statistical
              question
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Warm-Up --- Activate Prior Knowledge]{sky}
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.48\linewidth}
    \textbf{The three items and what each seeds}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{1.} Individuals, one variable, one value on a rental sheet.
              \textbf{Spirals} Lesson 1.0 and puts a table in front of them before the
              activity's table arrives.
        \item \textbf{2.} Which lists can you add up? Prices and ages yes; jersey numbers and
              house numbers no. \textbf{Seeds:} the amount test / EK VAR-1.C.2.
        \item \textbf{3.} ``Is your school ID number measuring anything about you?''
              \textbf{Seeds:} digits-as-label, in a context every student owns.
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.48\linewidth}
    \textbf{Running it}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Name nothing.} Do not say \emph{categorical} or \emph{quantitative} in
              the warm-up debrief --- those arrive after the activity. Write their own
              phrasings on the board (``it's an amount,'' ``it's just a name'') and leave them
              up; the debrief will circle them.
        \item Debrief item~2 aloud and take the ``what do they have in common'' answer from
              two different groups before moving on. That sentence is the whole lesson in
              student language.
        \item Item~3 usually lands instantly --- that instinct is the lever you pull in the
              debrief when a group insists Permit \# is a number.
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Experience \& Formalize --- The Activity: \emph{The Trailhead Register}]{sky}
\textbf{Launch (2 min).} Project the register. \emph{``Every party that hikes here signs in and
signs out, and the ranger keeps the sheet all season. Sort the columns with your group. I am not
giving you the piles --- you make them, and you name them.''} Groups of 3--4. Do not preview any
vocabulary.

\begin{multicols}{2}
\textbf{What students do.} \emph{Problem 1} makes them invent the sort themselves: 1b is the
sort, 1c makes them articulate the rule, 1d tests it with averaging (and forces the nonsense out
loud on \emph{Weather}), 1e seeds counting as the alternative summary --- which is Lesson 1.2.

\emph{Problem 2} breaks whatever rule they wrote. All four new columns are digits; only
\emph{Water bottles} is an amount. \textbf{2b is the crux:} the permit average computes cleanly
to 4179.5 and means nothing, while the bottle average means something. 2c makes them repair the
rule. 2d is the reverse trap --- a percent drawn out of a Yes/No column.

\columnbreak
\textbf{What the teacher does.} Circulate. Listen before speaking. When a group stalls, give a
\emph{question, cue, or prompt}, not an answer:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, nosep]
  \item \textbf{1b:} ``You put those three together. Say out loud what they have in common ---
        don't point, say it.''
  \item \textbf{1d:} ``Add the Weather column for me. Go ahead. What's Sunny plus Rain?''
  \item \textbf{2b (the crux):} ``Your calculator gave you 4179.5. Point at the party that
        had 4179.5. Now do the same for the 4 bottles.''
  \item \textbf{2d:} ``Read me the entries in the Dog column. Any numbers in there? Where did
        the 62 come from, then?''
\end{itemize}
While circulating, pick the work to display: one group's pile-naming sentence from 1c, and one
group that put \emph{Permit \#} in the amount pile in 2a --- open the debrief on that.
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Debrief --- Formalize (what goes on the board, in a second color)]{sky}
Work through the displayed student work in order and \textbf{attach each formal name to
something a group already wrote}. Students fill their QuickNotes line by line as you go.
\begin{multicols}{2}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.3em, nosep, itemsep=3pt]
  \item Their pile names from 1c $\to$ \emph{categorical} and \emph{quantitative}. Circle their
        own words first, then write the term beside them. Read the CED wording once: category
        names or group labels; numerical values for a measured or counted quantity.
  \item Restate \emph{variable} as the CED does --- a characteristic that \emph{changes} from
        one individual to another. This is the 1.0 definition sharpened, not a new one.
  \item 1d and 2b together $\to$ \textbf{the amount test}. Write it as the students said it:
        \emph{is this an amount of something, or the name of a group?} Say plainly that
        ``has digits'' is the wrong test and 4179.5 is the proof.
  \item 2d $\to$ the reverse trap. Summary numeric, variable categorical.
  \item Only then name what each kind is summarized \emph{with} --- counts and percents vs.
        averages and spread. That is the bridge to Lesson 1.2.
\end{enumerate}
\columnbreak
\textbf{QuickNotes.} Half a page, filled together. The mini table on the left is the register in
miniature --- point at the Trail column and the Time column while naming the two kinds. The
right-hand bullets are their sentences, tightened. \textbf{It is a summary of what they found,
not a new lecture}; if you are talking for more than 90 seconds without pointing at student
work, stop.

\textbf{Why this order.} The two kinds first (they built them), the sharpened definition of
variable second, the amount test third --- because the test is what \emph{separates} the two
kinds, so it only means something once both exist. Naming the test first would make it a rule to
memorize instead of a consequence.

\textbf{Pose cold:} \emph{``A satisfaction survey records a rating from 1 to 5. Which kind?''}
Let them argue --- it is genuinely contested, and hearing both sides proves they are using the
amount test rather than counting digits. Land it as \emph{categorical for our purposes}: the
1--5 are ordered labels, and the gap between 1 and 2 is not a measured amount.
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Application --- \emph{The Season Pass} (worked together, 6 min)]{sky}
The first place the just-named vocabulary is \emph{used}. Part (a) is the classification move on
a fresh context and carries two digit-labels (pass number, locker number) to catch anyone who
kept the old rule. \textbf{Part (b) is the real work:} binning age into age groups converts a
quantitative variable to a categorical one with no change to the underlying people. Insist that
someone name what is \emph{lost} --- the mean age. Part (c) is the crux restated in one line.

\textbf{Students hold the pen, you hold the questions:} \emph{``Is a locker number an amount of
anything?''} \quad \emph{``Same 3,400 people, same ages --- so what actually changed in
(b)?''} \quad \emph{``Can you get the average age back out of the four bands? Try it.''}

\emph{The \texttt{work} block lives in the activity (2b), not here:} the two averages are the
lesson's evidence, so they are computed where the discovery happens. The blank and the key stay
page-for-page through that block plus \texttt{\textbackslash answerspace}.
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Check Your Understanding --- in-class practice, \emph{not scored}]{redbox}
Five exercises spanning Topic 1.2, in new contexts: \textbf{1} classification on a coffee-shop
log (with a loyalty card number as the digit-label); \textbf{2} the \emph{deliberate contrast
pair} --- number of pets vs.\ type of pet, plus how each is summarized; \textbf{3} the binning
question in a medical context; \textbf{4} produce-your-own digit-label; \textbf{5} the AP-style
multiple-choice item. Pairs for 1--3, solo for 4--5.

\textbf{They carry no point value} --- the cover's score column reads \textbf{NA}. Do not collect
them for a grade. \textbf{Item~5 is the formative check:} four of the five options are digits, so
a student still counting digits has nothing to grab. Spot-check it on the way out and sort into
three piles --- chose (C) and justified it with ``minutes are an amount'' (ready); chose (C) with
a vague reason (ready, needs the language); chose any other option (the misconception survived).
If more than a third land in the last pile, reopen the amount test at the start of Lesson~1.2
rather than moving on. Eight minutes will not cover five items for everyone: \textbf{priority is
1, 3, and 5}; items~2 and~4 are the early-finisher bank. The graded practice is the DeltaMath
homework, below.
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[AP Practice --- AP-style practice (assigned, not scored)]{goldbox}
Four AP-style multiple-choice items (five options each, in context) on the trailhead register
with the ranger's new columns merged in, then one multi-part free-response set on a city
library: classify four variables, refute a meaningless average, explain what binning costs, and
write a statistical question about a categorical variable. \textbf{Part (d) is the spiral} --- it
sends students back to Lesson 1.0's two checks and makes them run those checks on a categorical
variable, which is where students most often assume ``statistical'' means ``numeric.'' The
optional extension is the classify-any-table hunt.

\textbf{Not scored --- the cover's score column reads \textbf{NA} for this page.} Go over it at
the start of Lesson~1.2 and hold the AP standard out loud: \emph{in context or it does not
count}. A part (a) that writes ``C, Q, C, Q'' without naming the variables is incomplete; a part
(c) that says ``it becomes categorical'' without naming the lost mean does not earn the point.
Students keep this page --- it is their AP-format rep, not a grade.
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Watch For (while circulating)]{redbox}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, nosep]
  \item \textbf{``It has digits, so it's a number''} (2a, Application~a, CYU~1, HW~2 --- the
        target misconception): \emph{Permit \#}, \emph{Home ZIP}, \emph{Parking spot},
        \emph{Loyalty card \#}. Probe: \emph{``Is that an amount of something? Point at the
        party that had 4179.5 of it.''}
  \item \textbf{Summary mistaken for the variable} (2d, HW~3 --- the reverse trap): the 62\%
        makes \emph{Dog along} look quantitative. Probe: \emph{``Read me what is actually
        written in that column.''}
  \item \textbf{Binning not recognized as a change of kind} (Application~b, CYU~3, HW~4):
        students say ``it's the same information, so it's still quantitative.'' Probe:
        \emph{``Get me the mean age out of those four bands.''}
  \item \textbf{Counting confused with categorizing} (1e, CYU~2): ``number of pets'' is
        quantitative, ``type of pet'' is not, even though both get counted somewhere. Probe:
        \emph{``What is written in each cell --- a count, or a name?''}
  \item \textbf{Currency and time doubted} (CYU~1, warm-up~2): some students hesitate on
        \emph{Price paid} because of the dollar sign. Probe: \emph{``Does \$4.50 plus \$3.25
        mean something?''}
\end{itemize}
Cold-call prompts: \emph{``Give me one categorical and one quantitative variable about the
people in this room.''} \quad \emph{``Name a variable written in digits that is categorical, and
say what the digits are doing.''}
\end{skillbox}

\textbf{Point students to DeltaMath} for the graded homework (due next class) and remind them the
AP Practice page in the packet is theirs to work and bring to review, then close on what changed
rather than on the assignment: \emph{``You walked in able to tell a word from a number. You walk

every statistic we use for the rest of the year.''} \textbf{Preview:} Lesson~1.2,
\emph{Representing a Categorical Variable} --- we take the pile you cannot average and count it

Topic 1.2, and a second split competes with the one distinction this lesson is built on.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{teachernote}[Warm-Up]
Item~2 is the seed and item~3 is the lever. Give four minutes total and do not let item~1 eat
the clock --- it is a spiral, not the point. Expect an argument on \textbf{(D) house numbers}:
some students will say house numbers ``go up as you walk down the street,'' which is true and
still does not make their total mean anything. Validate the ordering observation and ask what
the sum would be measuring --- that exchange is worth more than the right answer. Write their
``it's an amount'' / ``it's just a name'' phrasings on the board and leave them there; you will
circle them in the debrief. \textbf{Do not say \emph{categorical} or \emph{quantitative} here.}
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Experience \& Formalize]
\textbf{Activity (20 min).} Roughly 8 minutes on problem~1, 12 on problem~2 --- problem~2 carries
the crux and needs the time. Most groups sort problem~1 correctly in three minutes; the work is
1c, making them \emph{say} the rule. Push for a sentence, not a gesture. \textbf{The single
discipline that makes this lesson work: do not name anything while circulating.} If you say
``categorical'' at a table, that table stops discovering. Make sure every group actually computes
both averages in 2b --- a group that reasons about the permit average without computing it misses
the whole force of the moment, which is that the arithmetic \emph{works} and the answer is still
meaningless.

\textbf{Debrief (12 min).} The must-land moments are (1) their pile names becoming the two terms
and (3) the amount test; everything else can be compressed. Open on the group that filed
\emph{Permit \#} as an amount --- correcting it in public, gently, is what makes the crux stick.
If time is short, cut the 1--5 rating cold-call; it is genuinely contested and can swallow four
minutes.

\textbf{Application (6 min).} Part (b) is the point of the whole box; if you only get through (a)
and (b), that is the right trade. Students hold the pen.

\textbf{Check Your Understanding (8 min).} Not scored --- spot-check rather than collecting the
page. Priority items are 1, 3, and 5; 2 and 4 are the early-finisher bank. Item~2 is the
deliberate contrast pair and item~5 is the formative check that decides how Lesson~1.2 opens.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[AP Practice]
\textbf{This page is not scored} --- the graded homework for this lesson is the DeltaMath
assignment. Work it as AP-format reps and go over it at the start of Lesson~1.2. The trailhead
register is reused on purpose --- the context is already loaded, so the thinking stays on the
classification rather than on re-reading a table. MC item~1 is the baseline check, item~2 the
target misconception stated as a student's argument, item~3 the reverse trap, and item~4 binning.
\textbf{Item~3 is the one worth reading closely}: distractors (A) and (B) are both the ``it
produced a number'' error, and a student who picks either has not separated the summary from the
variable. On the free response, hold the line on context --- part (c) is where students first
learn that naming the change is only half the answer and naming the cost is the other half. The
extension pairs well with a five-minute share-out at the start of Lesson~1.2.
\end{teachernote}
