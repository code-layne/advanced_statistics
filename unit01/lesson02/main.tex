\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=sky, colframe=navy, boxrule=0.9pt, arc=2mm,
    left=2mm, right=2mm, top=1.5mm, bottom=1.5mm]
    \textbf{Primary Objective:} Students will build frequency and relative frequency tables for
    a categorical variable, read a bar graph of either one, and explain why two groups of
    different sizes can only be compared with relative frequencies.
    \\[2pt]
    \textbf{CED alignment:} Topics 1.3 \emph{and} 1.4 \quad$\cdot$\quad Big Idea: UNC
    (Uncertainty) \quad$\cdot$\quad Skills 2.A, 2.B, 2.D \quad$\cdot$\quad
    LO UNC-1.A--UNC-1.E \quad$\cdot$\quad EK UNC-1.A.1, 1.B.1, 1.B.2, 1.C.1--3, 1.D.1, 1.E.1
    \\[2pt]
    \textbf{Scope note:} this lesson absorbs \emph{two} CED topics (tables and graphs), per
    \texttt{COURSE\_RESTRUCTURE.md}. That is a lot for 55 minutes. The activity is built around
    the one idea both topics share --- \textbf{counts versus rates} --- and the bar-graph
    mechanics ride along on a single pre-drawn chart rather than getting their own block. If the
    period runs short, protect problem 2; problem 1 can be finished as homework.
    \\[2pt]
    \textbf{Lesson model:} \emph{Experience first, formalize later.} Students work the activity
    in small groups using only prior knowledge; the teacher circulates with questions, cues, and
    prompts (not answers); the debrief attaches the formal vocabulary to what the groups already
    found. \textbf{No vocabulary before the debrief.}
\end{tcolorbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Learning Targets \& Key Understandings]{goldbox}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X|X@{}}
\textbf{Learning targets (student language)} & \textbf{Key understandings (the ``why'')} \\[2pt]
\hline
\vspace{-6pt}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, nosep, after=\vspace{2pt}]
  \item I can turn a table of counts into a table of shares, and say what each column must
        add to.
  \item I can read a bar graph and say which number each bar shows.
  \item I can explain why comparing different-sized groups by counts is unfair.
  \item I can decide whether a ``most people'' claim is supported by the numbers.
\end{itemize}
&
\vspace{-6pt}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, nosep, after=\vspace{2pt}]
  \item A frequency table gives counts; a relative frequency table gives proportions
        (EK UNC-1.A.1). Percents, proportions, and rates carry identical information
        (EK UNC-1.B.1).
  \item \textbf{Target misconception:} that the group with the larger count is ``more'' on that
        category. South has \emph{more walkers} (150 vs.\ 80) and a \emph{lower walking rate}
        (15\% vs.\ 20\%). Both are true; the bigger group wins on counts before anyone counts
        anything (EK UNC-1.E.1).
  \item \textbf{Second misconception:} that the largest category is a majority. Bus is 45\% ---
        a plurality, not ``most.''
\end{itemize}
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[{Vocabulary, Concepts, \& Theorems --- attached in the debrief, not before}]{greenbox}
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.48\linewidth}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Frequency table} --- the number of cases in each category (EK UNC-1.A.1)
        \item \textbf{Relative frequency table} --- the proportion in each category; entries
              always sum to 1
        \item \textbf{Proportion / percent / rate} --- three presentations of one number
              (EK UNC-1.B.1)
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.48\linewidth}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Bar graph} --- bar height is the count or the relative frequency for a
              category; bars are separated by gaps (EK UNC-1.C.1, UNC-1.C.2)
        \item \textbf{Plurality vs.\ majority} --- largest share vs.\ more than half
        \item \textbf{Carried forward from Lesson 1.1} --- categorical vs.\ quantitative
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Warm-Up --- Activate Prior Knowledge]{sky}
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.48\linewidth}
    \textbf{The three items and what each seeds}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{1.} Classify species and weight. \textbf{Spirals} Lesson 1.1 and
              establishes that today's variable is the categorical one.
        \item \textbf{2.} $80/400$ and $150/1000$ as percents. \textbf{Seeds:} the exact
              arithmetic the crux needs, done before the crux so it is not the obstacle.
        \item \textbf{3.} Two teams, different numbers of games. \textbf{This is the whole
              lesson in prior-knowledge terms} --- every student already knows you compare
              records by rate.
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.48\linewidth}
    \textbf{Running it}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Name nothing.} Do not say \emph{relative frequency} or \emph{bar graph}
              in the warm-up debrief. Write their own phrasings on the board (``out of,''
              ``share,'' ``per game'') and leave them up.
        \item Debrief item~3 aloud and press the second half: \emph{why} the raw win totals did
              not decide it. That sentence is what you will circle in the debrief.
        \item Item~3 is the lever for any group that stalls on 2c --- send them back to it
              rather than explaining.
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Experience \& Formalize --- The Activity: \emph{Getting to School}]{sky}
\textbf{Launch (2 min).} \emph{``One district, two high schools, one question. Work the two
problems with your group. For every answer, be ready to tell me which number you used.''}
Groups of 3--4. Do not preview any vocabulary.

\begin{multicols}{2}
\textbf{What students do.} \emph{Problem 1} is the table toolkit on one school: 1a is the
percent column, 1b forces them to notice the two totals are different promises (400 vs.\ 100\%),
and \textbf{1d is the plurality trap} --- 45\% is the biggest group and is not ``most.''

\emph{Problem 2} is the contrast case. The district's side-by-side chart of raw counts sits
right above the questions, so students meet the misleading display before they are asked about
it. \textbf{2c is the crux:} South has more walkers and North has the higher rate, and students
must hold both, choose one for a stated purpose, and name the fix for the chart.

\columnbreak
\textbf{What the teacher does.} Circulate. Listen before speaking. When a group stalls, give a
\emph{question, cue, or prompt}, not an answer:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, nosep]
  \item \textbf{1b:} ``Add your percent column. What did you get? Would that still be the
        answer at South?''
  \item \textbf{1d:} ``How many students is 45\%? How many students is everyone else?''
  \item \textbf{2c (the crux):} ``Go back to the warm-up. Team B won more games. Did that make
        Team B better?''
  \item \textbf{2c, chart half:} ``Cover the Bike bars. Is there any category where South
        \emph{could} have lost?''
\end{itemize}
While circulating, pick the work to display: one group's 1b sentence, and one group that
answered 2a with a single school --- open the debrief on that.
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Debrief --- Formalize (what goes on the board, in a second color)]{sky}
Work through the displayed student work in order and \textbf{attach each formal name to
something a group already wrote}. Students fill their QuickNotes line by line as you go.
\begin{multicols}{2}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.3em, nosep, itemsep=3pt]
  \item Their two columns $\to$ \emph{frequency table} and \emph{relative frequency table}.
        Their 1b sentence is EK UNC-1.A.1 in student words --- circle it, then write the terms.
  \item Write $80/400 = 0.20 = 20\% =$ ``1 in 5'' on one line and name it once: proportion,
        percent, and rate are the same number (EK UNC-1.B.1).
  \item Now the bar graph. Point at the posted chart: each bar is a category, the height is the
        count \emph{or} the proportion depending on the axis, and the gaps are there because
        categories are not a number line. This is the only direct instruction in the lesson ---
        keep it under two minutes.
  \item 2c $\to$ the crux. Write both true statements side by side, then the rule: comparing
        different-sized groups requires relative frequencies (EK UNC-1.E.1).
  \item 1d $\to$ \emph{plurality} vs.\ \emph{majority}. Land it as a reading habit, not a
        vocabulary item.
\end{enumerate}
\columnbreak
\textbf{QuickNotes.} Half a page, filled together. \textbf{It is a summary of what they found,
not a new lecture}; if you are talking for more than 90 seconds without pointing at student
work, stop.

\textbf{Why this order.} Tables first (they built them), the three-names-for-one-number line
second so the crux has vocabulary to use, graphs third, and the comparison rule last --- because
the rule is what the graph made visible. Naming the rule first would turn the chart into an
illustration instead of the evidence.

\textbf{Pose cold:} \emph{``Which shift is busier?''} from the Application. It is the one place
in this lesson where the \emph{count} is the right answer. Students who over-learn ``always use
rates'' need this, and it prevents the rule from becoming a reflex.
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Application --- \emph{Two Shifts} (worked together, 6 min)]{sky}
The first place the vocabulary is \emph{used}. Part (a) is the crux in a new context, and the
numbers are built to reverse: morning sells more large drinks (84 vs.\ 32) while evening sells
them at the higher rate (40\% vs.\ 35\%). Part (b) demands a sentence with the number named.
\textbf{Part (c) is the counterweight:} ``which shift is busier'' is answered by the total ---
a count. Say plainly that the lesson is not ``rates are always right''; it is ``say which number
you used and why.''

\textbf{Students hold the pen, you hold the questions:} \emph{``Which shift sold more? Which
sells them more often? Are those the same question?''} \quad \emph{``Busier --- is that about
how many, or about what share?''}

\emph{The \texttt{work} block lives in the activity (2b):} the two walking rates are the
lesson's evidence, so they are computed where the discovery happens.
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Check Your Understanding --- in-class practice, \emph{not scored}]{redbox}
Five exercises spanning Topics 1.3--1.4: \textbf{1} percent plus the ``most'' check;
\textbf{2} the \emph{crux again} in a medical context, with both rates required;
\textbf{3} the same data plotted as counts and as proportions --- what changed and what did not
(EK UNC-1.C.1); \textbf{4} four percents adding to 95\%, a structural check; \textbf{5} the
AP-style multiple-choice item. Pairs for 1--3, solo for 4--5.

\textbf{They carry no point value} --- the cover's score column reads \textbf{NA}. Do not collect
them for a grade. \textbf{Item~5 is the formative check:} the distractors are the two halves of
the crux stated separately, so a student who has only half the idea will pick (A) or (B). Spot
check on the way out and sort into three piles --- chose (C) with both rates computed (ready);
chose (C) by elimination (ready, needs the arithmetic); chose (A) or (B) (the misconception
survived). If more than a third land in the last pile, reopen the two-schools table at the start
of Lesson~1.3. Eight minutes will not cover five items: \textbf{priority is 2, 4, and 5}; items
1 and 3 are the early-finisher bank.
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[AP Practice --- AP-style practice (assigned, not scored)]{goldbox}
Four AP-style multiple-choice items on a city recycling audit --- a rate computation, the crux
stated as four competing claims, the structural must-sum-to-1 fact, and a display-choice item
(EK UNC-1.E.1) --- then one multi-part free-response set on a 9th-versus-12th-grade activity
survey: identify and classify, build a relative frequency table with the arithmetic shown,
correct a newspaper's count-based comparison, and describe the largest difference in context.
\textbf{Part (a) is the spiral} --- it sends students back to Lesson 1.1 to classify the variable
before they are allowed to summarize it, which is the habit that keeps them from averaging a
categorical column later.

\textbf{Not scored --- the cover's score column reads \textbf{NA} for this page.} Go over it at
the start of Lesson~1.3 and hold the AP standard out loud: \emph{in context or it does not
count}. A part (b) with three decimals and no context earns nothing; a part (d) that says
``study hall is different'' without the two percents is incomplete.
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Watch For (while circulating)]{redbox}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, nosep]
  \item \textbf{Counts compared across different-sized groups} (2c, CYU~2, HW~2 --- the target
        misconception). Probe: \emph{``Team B won more games. Was Team B better?''}
  \item \textbf{Plurality read as majority} (1d, CYU~1). Probe: \emph{``45\% chose the bus. What
        did the other 55\% do?''}
  \item \textbf{Percent taken of the wrong total} --- dividing by 750 (both neighborhoods) or by
        the category rather than the group. Probe: \emph{``Percent \emph{of what}? Point at the
        number you divided by.''}
  \item \textbf{Relative frequencies not summing to 1} (CYU~4, HW~3): students treat the column
        as four unrelated numbers. Probe: \emph{``Add your column. What should it come to, and
        why must it?''}
  \item \textbf{Rate applied where a count is wanted} (Application~c): over-correction after the
        crux lands. Probe: \emph{``Busier --- how many, or what share?''}
  \item \textbf{Answers with no context:} ``20\%'' alone. Probe: \emph{``20\% of whom, doing
        what?''} Hold this from day one; it is the AP scoring line.
\end{itemize}
Cold-call prompts: \emph{``Give me one number from this table that is a count and one that is a
rate.''} \quad \emph{``What would make this chart a fair comparison?''}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Close \& Preview]{goldbox}
\textbf{Point students to DeltaMath} for the graded homework (due next class) and remind them the
AP Practice page is theirs to work and bring to review, then close on what changed: \emph{``You
walked in able to read a table. You walk out able to catch a chart that is technically accurate
and still misleading --- which is most of the charts you will ever be shown.''}
\textbf{Preview:} Lesson~1.3, \emph{Representing a Quantitative Variable with Graphs} ---
counting worked today because there were four category names. When the values are numbers that
barely repeat, counting stops working and we need different displays. Note that
\emph{segmented bar charts} and \emph{pie charts} are named in the CED (EK UNC-1.C.3) but are
deliberately \textbf{not} built here; they return in Unit 2 with two-way tables, where they
actually earn their place.
\end{skillbox}

% ── Teacher notes — one per component, in packet order ────────────────────────
\begin{teachernote}[Warm-Up]
Item~3 is the one that matters --- it is the entire lesson in language every student already
has. Give four minutes and make sure the second half gets answered out loud: \emph{why} Team B's
larger win total did not decide it. Write that sentence on the board verbatim and leave it up;
you will point at it during the debrief and again at any table stuck on 2c. Item~2 exists so the
arithmetic is not the obstacle later --- if students are slow on percents, spend the extra
minute here rather than inside the activity. \textbf{Do not say \emph{relative frequency} or
\emph{bar graph} in this debrief.}
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Experience \& Formalize]
\textbf{Activity (20 min).} Roughly 7 minutes on problem~1, 13 on problem~2. Problem~1 goes
fast; the trap is 1d, and groups that blow past it will repeat the error in CYU~1, so make them
say the percent out loud. \textbf{The single discipline that makes this lesson work: do not name
anything while circulating.} A group that hears ``relative frequency'' stops discovering. Send
them to the warm-up instead.

\textbf{This lesson covers two CED topics, so the debrief is the tightest of the unit.} The
bar-graph block (debrief step 3) is the only stretch of direct instruction in the lesson --- keep
it under two minutes and keep pointing at the posted chart. If you are running long, cut the
plurality/majority naming and pick it up in CYU~1 review.

\textbf{Debrief (12 min).} Must-land: their two columns becoming the two table names, and 2c
becoming the comparison rule. Open on the group that answered 2a with only one school ---
correcting it gently in public is what makes the crux stick.

\textbf{Application (6 min).} Part (c) is the counterweight and is easy to skip when time is
short. Do not skip it: without it students leave with ``always use rates,'' which is wrong.

\textbf{Check Your Understanding (8 min).} Not scored --- spot-check rather than collecting.
Priority items are 2, 4, and 5; 1 and 3 are the early-finisher bank.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[AP Practice]
\textbf{This page is not scored} --- the graded homework is the DeltaMath assignment. Work it as
AP-format reps and go over it at the start of Lesson~1.3. \textbf{MC item~2 is the one worth
reading closely}: options (A) and (B) are each half of the truth, and students who pick either
have not held both facts at once. On the free response, part (b) is where students first have to
\emph{show} relative frequency arithmetic rather than state a result --- hold the line on
writing the division, since the AP exam awards the setup. Part (c) is the highest-value part:
it asks them to correct someone else's statistic, which is the form this idea takes on the exam
and in every news story they will read.
\end{teachernote}
