\boxguard[14]
\begin{objectivebox}
\small
By the end of these notes I will be able to:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em, nosep, after=\vspace{2pt}]
  \item build a \textbf{frequency} and a \textbf{relative frequency} table for a categorical
        variable, and say what each column has to add to;
  \item read a bar graph and say exactly which number each bar height is showing;
  \item explain why two groups of different sizes can only be compared with relative
        frequencies --- and when a count is still the right number.
\end{enumerate}
\end{objectivebox}

\vspace{0.10in}

\boxguard[16]
\begin{vocabbox}
\small
Fill in each term \textbf{as we name it} in the notes below.
\par
\termans{Frequency table}{a table of the}{number of cases in each category; the counts add to the group size.}
\par
\termans{Relative frequency table}{a table of the share}{of the group in each category; the entries always add to 1, or 100\%.}
\par
\termans{Bar graph}{one bar per category,}{its height the count or the relative frequency; gaps separate the bars.}
\par
\termans{The comparison rule}{to compare groups of}{different sizes, use relative frequencies --- never raw counts.}
\par
\end{vocabbox}

% ── I DO: section 1 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\boxguard[20]
\begin{notesbox}{1. Two Tables from One Column}
\small
A categorical column cannot be averaged --- last lesson settled that. What it \emph{can} be is
\textbf{counted}. North High asked all \textbf{400} of its students how they usually get to
school.

\vspace{6pt}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} l Y Y @{}}
\rowcolor{sky}
\textbf{How they get to school} & \textbf{Frequency} (count) &
\textbf{Relative frequency} \\
\hline
Bus  & 180 & \ans{0.45 \quad (45\%)} \\
Car  & 120 & \ans{0.30 \quad (30\%)} \\
Walk &  80 & \ans{0.20 \quad (20\%)} \\
Bike &  20 & \ans{0.05 \quad (5\%)} \\
\hline
\textbf{Total} & \textbf{400} & \ans{1.00 \quad (100\%)} \\
\end{tabularx}

\vspace{8pt}
A \textbf{frequency table} lists how many cases fall in each category; a \textbf{relative
frequency table} lists the share of the group in each category. \textbf{The two totals are
different promises.} Frequencies add to the \ans{group size} --- here, 400 students. Relative
frequencies always add to \ans{1 (or 100\%)}, because every student is counted in exactly
\ans{one} category.

\vspace{6pt}
\textbf{``Most'' is a promise about half.} A flyer reads \emph{``Most North students take the
bus.''} Bus is the largest single category at 45\% --- that makes it a \textbf{plurality}.
A \textbf{majority} would need more than \ans{half (50\%)} of the group. Bus does not have it: the
other three categories together come to \ans{55\%}.
\end{notesbox}

\vspace{0.10in}

% ── I DO: section 2 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\boxguard[16]
\begin{notesbox}{2. Proportion, Percent, Rate --- Three Names for One Number}
\small
Eighty of North's 400 students walk. Every line below says that same fact:

\vspace{4pt}
\begin{center}
\fcolorbox{navy}{sky}{\parbox{0.92\linewidth}{\centering
$\dfrac{80}{400} \;=\; 0.20 \;=\; 20\% \;=\;$ 1 in 5}}
\end{center}

\vspace{6pt}
The decimal is the \ans{proportion}; the 20\% is the \ans{percent}. Percentages, proportions,
and rates all carry the \ans{same} information, so use whichever your reader will
understand fastest.

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{On the AP exam, always attach the context:} not ``20\%,'' but \emph{20\% \textbf{of
North students} walk to school}.
\end{notesbox}

\vspace{0.10in}

% ── I DO: section 3 — the display ────────────────────────────────────────────
\boxguard[30]
\begin{notesbox}{3. Bar Graphs: What the Height Is}
\small
The same North High column, drawn:

\vspace{2pt}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.72]
  \draw[linegray, very thin] (0,1) -- (5.4,1);
  \draw[linegray, very thin] (0,2) -- (5.4,2);
  \draw[linegray, very thin] (0,3) -- (5.4,3);
  \draw[->, thick] (0,0) -- (0,4.1);
  \draw[->, thick] (0,0) -- (5.6,0);
  \foreach \y/\lab in {1/50, 2/100, 3/150} {
    \draw (-0.08,\y) -- (0.08,\y) node[left, font=\tiny, xshift=-2pt] {\lab};}
  \foreach \x/\h/\lab in {0.5/3.6/Bus, 1.8/2.4/Car, 3.1/1.6/Walk, 4.4/0.4/Bike} {
    \fill[navy] (\x,0) rectangle ++(0.8,\h);
    \node[below, font=\tiny] at (\x+0.4,-0.05) {\lab};}
  \node[rotate=90, font=\tiny] at (-0.75,2.0) {Students};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\vspace{2pt}
\vspace{6pt}
\textbf{Read it.} The \emph{Walk} bar reaches \ans{80} students, which is \ans{20\%} of
North's 400. Add the four bar heights and you are back to \ans{400} --- a bar graph of
counts carries exactly the information the frequency table carried.

Each bar stands for one \ans{category}, and its height is the \ans{count} in that category.
The bars are drawn with \ans{gaps} between them, because the categories are separate groups
and not points on a number line --- which is also why the order of the bars carries no meaning.

\vspace{6pt}
\textbf{Relabel the vertical axis with relative frequencies} --- 0.45, 0.30, 0.20, 0.05 --- and
every bar keeps the height it already has. Only the \ans{axis labels} change. Reading a bar graph
therefore starts with one question: \emph{what is the vertical axis counting?}
\end{notesbox}

\vspace{0.10in}

% ── I DO: section 4 — the trap ───────────────────────────────────────────────
\boxguard[30]
\begin{notesbox}{4. The Bigger Group Wins Before Anyone Counts}
\small
South High is in the same district and surveyed all \textbf{1{,}000} of its students. The
district posted the chart on the right to compare the two schools.

\vspace{4pt}
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.46\linewidth}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} l Y Y Y @{}}
\rowcolor{sky}
\textbf{School} & \textbf{Walk} & \textbf{Bike} & \textbf{Total} \\
\hline
North &  80 & 20 &   400 \\
South & 150 & 20 & 1{,}000 \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.50\linewidth}
\vspace{-6pt}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.62]
  \draw[linegray, very thin] (0,1) -- (6.6,1);
  \draw[linegray, very thin] (0,2) -- (6.6,2);
  \draw[linegray, very thin] (0,3) -- (6.6,3);
  \draw[->, thick] (0,0) -- (0,3.8);
  \draw[->, thick] (0,0) -- (6.8,0);
  \foreach \y/\lab in {1/200, 2/400, 3/600} {
    \draw (-0.08,\y) -- (0.08,\y) node[left, font=\tiny, xshift=-2pt] {\lab};}
  \foreach \x/\hn/\hs/\lab in {0.4/0.9/2.5/Bus, 2.0/0.6/1.65/Car, 3.6/0.4/0.75/Walk,
                               5.2/0.1/0.1/Bike} {
    \fill[navy]   (\x,0)     rectangle ++(0.55,\hn);
    \fill[goldacc](\x+0.6,0) rectangle ++(0.55,\hs);
    \node[below, font=\tiny] at (\x+0.57,-0.05) {\lab};}
  \fill[navy] (5.9,3.3) rectangle ++(0.28,0.28);
  \node[right, font=\tiny] at (6.2,3.44) {North};
  \fill[goldacc] (5.9,2.9) rectangle ++(0.28,0.28);
  \node[right, font=\tiny] at (6.2,3.04) {South};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\end{minipage}

\vspace{4pt}
South's bar is taller in almost every category --- and that was guaranteed before anyone counted
anything, because South surveyed \ans{2.5 times} as many students. Work the two walking rates:

\begin{work}
\text{North walking rate} &= \frac{80}{400} = 0.20 = 20\% \\[4pt]
\text{South walking rate} &= \frac{150}{1000} = 0.15 = 15\%
\end{work}

South has \ans{more} walkers, 150 against 80. North has the \ans{higher} walking rate,
20\% against 15\%. \textbf{Both statements are true}, and they answer two different questions.

\vspace{6pt}
\begin{center}
\fcolorbox{navy}{sky}{\parbox{0.92\linewidth}{\centering\small
\textbf{The comparison rule.} To compare groups of different sizes, use\\
\ans{relative frequencies}, never raw counts.}}
\end{center}

\vspace{6pt}
The fix for the district's chart is one change: relabel the vertical axis so each bar shows the
\ans{percent} of that school rather than a head count.

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Counts are not always wrong.} ``How many students in this district walk to school?'' is
a question about \ans{how many}, and the count is exactly the right number. The rule is not
``always use rates'' --- it is \emph{say which number you used, and why that one}.
\end{notesbox}

% ── WE DO: guided practice ───────────────────────────────────────────────────
\boxguard[26]
\begin{practicebox}
\small
\textbf{Two Shifts --- we work this one together.} A coffee shop records the size of every drink
it sells. The morning shift filled \textbf{240} orders; the evening shift filled \textbf{80}.

\vspace{4pt}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} l Y Y Y Y @{}}
\rowcolor{sky}
\textbf{Shift} & \textbf{Small} & \textbf{Medium} & \textbf{Large} & \textbf{Total} \\
\hline
Morning &  48 & 108 & 84 & 240 \\
Evening &  18 &  30 & 32 &  80 \\
\hline
\end{tabularx}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\alph*.}, leftmargin=1.8em, itemsep=7pt]
  \item Which shift sold more large drinks? \ans{Morning (84)} \quad Now work out each shift's
        large-drink rate.
        \begin{work}
        \text{Morning} &= \frac{84}{240} = 0.35 = 35\% \\[4pt]
        \text{Evening} &= \frac{32}{80} = 0.40 = 40\%
        \end{work}
        Which shift sells them at the higher rate? \ans{Evening (40\%)}
  \item The owner wants one sentence for the staff newsletter comparing what the two shifts
        sell. Write it, and say which kind of number you used and why.\\[3pt]
        \ansline{Evening sells large drinks at the higher rate: 40\% of its orders versus the morning's 35\%.}
        \ansline{A rate --- the two shifts filled very different numbers of orders (80 versus 240).}
  \item \textbf{The question changes.} The owner now asks, ``Which shift is \emph{busier}?''
        Which column answers that --- and is the answer a rate or a count?\\[3pt]
        \ansline{The Total column answers it: morning filled 240 orders and evening only 80, so morning.}
        \ansline{That is a count. ``Busier'' asks how many orders, not what share of them were large.}
\end{enumerate}
\end{practicebox}

% ── YOU DO: independent practice ─────────────────────────────────────────────
\boxguard[24]
\begin{notesbox}{Independent Practice --- You Try}
\small
Work these on your own. I will circulate.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.9em, itemsep=8pt]
  \item The drama club has \textbf{40} members and \textbf{18} of them made honor roll. The band
        has \textbf{100} members and \textbf{30} of them made honor roll.

        \vspace{3pt}
        More members on honor roll: \ans{the band (30)} \quad Higher honor-roll rate:
        \ans{drama (45\%)}

        \vspace{3pt}
        The principal wants to say which club is doing better academically. Which of those two
        numbers should she use, and why that one?\\[3pt]
        \ansline{The rate. The clubs are very different sizes, so counts favor the band automatically;}
        \ansline{45\% of drama versus 30\% of band is the fair comparison of academic performance.}
  \item A poll of 250 students on a spirit-week theme: Decades 100, Superheroes 90, Neon 60.
        The newspaper prints ``Most students chose Decades.'' Is that supported? Explain.\\[3pt]
        \ansline{No. Decades is the largest share at 100/250 = 40\%, a plurality rather than a majority.}
        \ansline{``Most'' would need more than 125 students; the other 150 chose something else.}
  \item A student wants North's walking rate. He divides North's 80 walkers by 1{,}400 --- the
        two schools combined --- and reports 5.7\%. Say what question his number actually
        answers, and what he should have divided by instead.\\[3pt]
        \ansline{His 5.7\% is the share of all 1,400 surveyed students who both walk and attend North.}
        \ansline{North's walking rate needs North's own total: $80/400 = 20\%$.}
\end{enumerate}
\end{notesbox}
