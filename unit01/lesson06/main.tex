    \textbf{Primary Objective:} Students will assemble the five-number summary for a quantitative
    variable, read a boxplot as four sections each holding a quarter of the data, and compare two
    or more groups on center, variability, and unusual features --- in context, with explicit
    comparative language.
    \\[2pt]
    \textbf{CED alignment:} Topics 1.8 and 1.9 \quad$\cdot$\quad Big Idea: UNC (Uncertainty)
    \quad$\cdot$\quad Skills 2.A, 2.B, 2.D \quad$\cdot$\quad LO UNC-1.L, UNC-1.M, UNC-1.N,
    UNC-1.O \quad$\cdot$\quad EK UNC-1.L.1, 1.L.2, 1.M.1, 1.M.2, 1.N.1, 1.O.1
    \\[2pt]
    \textbf{Lesson model:} \emph{Experience first, formalize later.} Students work the activity
    in small groups using only prior knowledge; the teacher circulates with questions, cues, and
    prompts (not answers); the debrief attaches the formal vocabulary to what the groups already
    found. \textbf{No vocabulary before the debrief.}
    \\[2pt]
    \textbf{Note on the ``no sketch'' rule:} \textbf{students never draw a boxplot in this
    lesson.} Every display is pre-drawn. Where a question says ``construct,'' the construction is
    filling in a table of five numbers --- never plotting an axis. Drawing boxplots by hand eats
    the period; the exam asks students to \emph{read} and \emph{compare} them.
    \\[2pt]
    \textbf{Note on the merge:} this period carries Topic 1.8 (build and read one boxplot) and
    Topic 1.9 (compare two or more) together. Protect the activity's 20 minutes and let the
    comparison reps live in the Application and the homework --- do not add a third activity
    problem.
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Learning Targets \& Key Understandings]{goldbox}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}X|X@{}}
\textbf{Learning targets (student language)} & \textbf{Key understandings (the ``why'')} \\[2pt]
\hline
\vspace{-6pt}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, nosep, after=\vspace{2pt}]
  \item I can pull five key numbers out of a data set and find them in a picture built from them.
  \item I can say what a wide part of that picture does and does not tell me.
  \item I can decide whether one value sits far enough from the rest to be set apart.
  \item I can compare two groups on where they sit, how spread out they are, and what is
        unusual --- in the setting of the problem.
\end{itemize}
&
\vspace{-6pt}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, nosep, after=\vspace{2pt}]
  \item The minimum, $Q_1$, the median, $Q_3$, and the maximum are the \textbf{five-number
        summary}; a boxplot is that summary drawn to scale (EK UNC-1.L.1, 1.L.2).
  \item \textbf{Target misconception:} that a wide section of a boxplot holds more
        observations. \textbf{Every section holds the same quarter of the data.} Width reports
        spread, not count --- and this is the single most common boxplot error on the exam.
  \item \textbf{Second misconception:} that comparing two groups means saying which median is
        higher. The CED wants center, variability, \emph{and} unusual features, each with a
        comparative word and each in context (EK UNC-1.N.1, 1.O.1).
  \item Skew moves the mean off the median: right-skew pulls the mean right of it, left-skew
        pulls it left (EK UNC-1.M.2).
\end{itemize}
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

    \end{itemize}

\begin{minipage}[t]{0.48\linewidth}

        \item \textbf{Modified boxplot} --- outliers plotted with their own symbol beyond the

        \item \textbf{Parallel (side-by-side) boxplots} --- one plot per group on a shared axis
              (EK UNC-1.N.1)
        \item \textbf{Mean vs.\ median position} --- symmetric: close; right-skewed: mean to the

    \end{itemize}

\begin{skillbox}[Warm-Up --- Activate Prior Knowledge]{sky}
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.48\linewidth}
    \textbf{The three items and what each seeds}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{1.} Nine ordered values; report the least, $Q_1$, the median, $Q_3$, the
              greatest, and the IQR. \textbf{Spirals} Lesson 1.5 --- and it is exactly the table
              the activity's 1a asks them to fill, done once with easy numbers.
        \item \textbf{2.} Count twelve values into three stated stretches. \textbf{Seeds:} the
              counting move the crux in 1b depends on, made mechanical first.
        \item \textbf{3.} One $1.5\times$IQR check with the arithmetic shown. \textbf{Seeds:}
              problem~2c, where the rule has to be \emph{recovered} rather than recited.
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.48\linewidth}
    \textbf{Running it}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Name nothing.} Do not say \emph{five-number summary}, \emph{boxplot},
              \emph{box}, \emph{whisker}, or \emph{parallel boxplots} in the warm-up debrief.
              Item~1 produces five numbers; call them ``these five numbers.''
        \item Item~2 is a drill --- 60 seconds. It exists so that counting is not the obstacle
              inside the activity, where the count is the whole discovery.
        \item \textbf{Leave item~3's arithmetic on the board for the rest of the period.}

\begin{skillbox}[Experience \& Formalize --- The Activity: \emph{Twenty Free Throws} and \emph{Two Bus Routes}]{sky}
\textbf{Launch (2 min).} \emph{``Twenty players, twenty numbers, and somebody drew a summary
picture from them. Your job in problem~1 is to work out what that picture is made of --- and then
to count. Problem~2 hands you two of those pictures and one commuter who has to decide.''}
Groups of 3--4. Do not preview any vocabulary.

\begin{multicols}{2}
\textbf{What students do.} \emph{Problem 1} is the discovery. 1a is a table fill straight out of
Lesson 1.5 --- least, $Q_1$, median, $Q_3$, greatest --- followed by locating each of those five
numbers in the picture below the dotplot. \textbf{1b is the crux:} they count the 20 dots falling
into each of the picture's four sections and get \textbf{5, 5, 5, 5}, even though the right-hand
section is more than five times as wide as the left-hand one. 1c makes them answer a classmate
who reads width as count. 1d names what the picture threw away.

\emph{Problem 2} carries Topic 1.9. Two pictures on a shared axis with the five numbers given;
2a computes the middle-half width for each route (5 minutes and 12 minutes), 2b makes the
comparison, \textbf{2c recovers the $1.5\times$IQR convention} from the lone dot at 58 minutes,
and 2d demands one recommendation defended from both pictures.

\columnbreak
\textbf{What the teacher does.} Circulate. Listen before speaking. When a group stalls, give a
\emph{question, cue, or prompt}, not an answer:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, nosep]
  \item \textbf{1a:} ``You wrote 34.5. Put your finger on 34.5 on the axis. Now slide it down.
        What is there?''
  \item \textbf{1b (the crux):} ``Do not estimate --- count. Read me the four numbers.''
        Then: ``Twenty dots, four sections, and you got 5, 5, 5, 5. Was that luck?''
  \item \textbf{1c:} ``How many players are in the wide section? How many in the narrow one? So
        what \emph{is} the wide one telling you?''
  \item \textbf{2c:} ``How wide is Route B's middle half? Now how far past the box is the lone
        rider? How many middle-half widths is that?''
  \item \textbf{2d:} ``You said Route A. A commuter can read `A is better' on a poster. What can
        they read \emph{only} from these two pictures?''
\end{itemize}
While circulating, pick the work to display: one group's 1b count row, and one group's 1c
sentence --- open the debrief on those two.
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Debrief --- Formalize (what goes on the board, in a second color)]{sky}
Work through the displayed student work in order and \textbf{attach each formal name to
something a group already wrote}. Students fill their QuickNotes line by line as you go.
\begin{multicols}{2}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.3em, nosep, itemsep=3pt]
  \item Their 1a table $\to$ the \emph{five-number summary} (EK UNC-1.L.1). Five numbers they
        already knew how to find; today they get a collective name.
  \item Their 1a locating answers $\to$ the \emph{boxplot} (EK UNC-1.L.2). Name the parts on the
        picture they were just pointing at: the \emph{box} runs $Q_1$ to $Q_3$, the line inside
        is the \emph{median}, the \emph{whiskers} reach out to the extremes.
  \item \textbf{Their 1b count row $\to$ the crux.} Write \textbf{5 \quad 5 \quad 5 \quad 5} on
        the board and above it \textbf{25\% \quad 25\% \quad 25\% \quad 25\%}. Then the rule:
        \emph{width shows spread, never count}. This is the step the rest of the year leans on.
  \item Problem~2's lone dot $\to$ \emph{outlier symbol} and the \emph{modified boxplot}
        (EK UNC-1.L.2): the whisker stops at the most extreme value that is \emph{not} an
        outlier. Point at their 2c arithmetic: $32 + 1.5(12) = 50$, and $58 > 50$.
  \item Problem~2's shared axis $\to$ \emph{parallel (side-by-side) boxplots}, and the
        \emph{comparison checklist} (EK UNC-1.N.1, 1.O.1): center, variability, unusual features,
        each with a comparative word, each in context.
  \item Last, in one line: \emph{mean vs.\ median position} (EK UNC-1.M.2). Point at problem~1's
        right-skewed picture --- the mean of those 20 sits above the median of 39.5.
  \item Close the box by naming its \textbf{cost}: gaps, clusters, and a second peak all
        disappear inside it. That is their own 1d answer, given its formal weight.
\end{enumerate}
\columnbreak
\textbf{QuickNotes.} Half a page, filled together. \textbf{It is a summary of what they found,
not a new lecture}; if you are talking for more than 90 seconds without pointing at student
work, stop.

\textbf{Why this order.} The five numbers first (they already own them), the picture second
\emph{as a drawing of those numbers}, the crux third --- because it only lands once students
accept the picture is nothing but five numbers placed on a line. Outliers and comparison come
after, because both are moves made \emph{on} a boxplot rather than facts about one. The
mean/median rule is a one-liner and goes late; it belongs to the shape work of 1.4 and only needs
attaching here.

\textbf{The sentence to land:} \emph{``Every section of a boxplot holds the same quarter of the
data. Width tells you how stretched out those values are --- it never tells you how many there
are.''} Say it, write it, and point at the 5 \quad 5 \quad 5 \quad 5 while you do.
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Application --- \emph{Two Science Sections} (worked together, 6 min)]{sky}
The first place the comparison vocabulary is \emph{used}. Two parallel boxplots of test scores
for two sections of the same course, five-number summaries given. Part (a) reads and compares
the centers and the middle-half widths (Period 2: median 80, IQR 12; Period 5: median 72,
IQR 16). Part (b) is EK UNC-1.M.2 read off the picture --- Period~2 is skewed left (a long lower
whisker), Period~5 skewed right --- and asks how the picture tells you.
\textbf{Part (c) is the AP-scored form:} one paragraph comparing the two sections, in context,
with explicit comparative language.

\textbf{Hold the standard out loud.} A paragraph that describes Period 2 and then describes
Period 5 \textbf{earns nothing} on an AP comparison item, no matter how accurate each half is.
The words that earn the point are \emph{higher than}, \emph{more variable than}, \emph{more
spread out than} --- and every one of them must name the variable (test score) and the groups
(the two sections).

\textbf{Students hold the pen, you hold the questions:} \emph{``You wrote `Period 5 has an IQR of
16.' Compared with what?''} \quad \emph{``Which side of Period 2's box is longer? So which way
does it trail off?''}

\emph{The \texttt{work} block in this lesson lives in the homework} (item~3, the $1.5\times$IQR
arithmetic), authored byte-identically in the blank and the key. Page lockstep everywhere else
rests on \texttt{\textbackslash answerspace}.
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[AP Practice --- AP-style practice (assigned, not scored)]{goldbox}
Four AP-style multiple-choice items on parallel boxplots of daily tips for weekday and weekend
shifts, then one multi-part free response on the same display. The four MC items are chosen to
cover the four ways this topic is tested: \textbf{1} is the width-means-count distractor with a
fully plausible wrong option; \textbf{2} is a five-number-summary reading and IQR item;
\textbf{3} is mean-versus-median position under right skew (EK UNC-1.M.2); and \textbf{4} is the
item whose correct answer is that \textbf{the boxplot cannot answer the question} --- whether
the weekend distribution is bimodal. That last one is a real AP item type and the honest limit of
the display; students who have internalized ``the boxplot shows the shape'' pick a wrong option
confidently.

Free response: report the weekday five-number summary, compute the weekend IQR and apply the
$1.5\times$IQR rule to \$158, compare the two shifts in context, and \textbf{part (d) is the
spiral} --- name the display you would ask for instead and say what it would show, which reaches
back to Lessons 1.3 and 1.4.

\textbf{Not scored --- the cover's score column reads \textbf{NA} for this page.} Go over it at
the start of Lesson~1.7 and hold the AP standard out loud: \emph{in context, and comparative, or
it does not count}.
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Homework --- the graded practice, started in class]{redbox}
Five exercises spanning both merged topics: \textbf{1} build the five-number summary from an
ordered list and match it to one of three pre-drawn boxplots --- the cheapest diagnostic in the
set, because a student who cannot place the median cannot do anything else today;
\textbf{2} the crux head-on, a student claiming the long right whisker means most values are
large; \textbf{3} the $1.5\times$IQR rule with the arithmetic in a \texttt{work} block;
\textbf{4} a full two-group comparison in context from parallel boxplots; \textbf{5} the AP-style
multiple-choice item with a one-sentence justification. Pairs for 1--3, solo for 4--5.

\textbf{This is the lesson's scored work} --- the cover's score column carries a blank for it and
it is collected next class. Students begin it in the last eight minutes of the period so that the
questions get asked while you are still in the room; they finish it at home.

\textbf{Eight minutes will not finish five items --- that is by design.} Point them at 1, 2,
and~3 in class, where a wrong start is cheap to catch; 4 and~5 travel home cleanly.
\textbf{Item~2 is where the misconception shows.} \textbf{Item~4 is where the comparison standard
shows} --- when you score it, separate ``wrote two descriptions'' from ``wrote a comparison,'' and
give no credit for the first even when both descriptions are right. If more than a third of the
class lands in the first pile, reopen the Application's paragraph in the Lesson~1.7 warm-up
debrief rather than letting it ride into inference.
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Watch For (while circulating)]{redbox}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, nosep]
  \item \textbf{``Most of the players are in the wide part''} (1b/1c --- the target
        misconception). Probe: \emph{``Count them. How many dots? Now count the narrow section.
        Say that out loud.''}
  \item \textbf{Estimating the counts instead of counting} (1b, HW~2). The discovery only lands
        if the four numbers come out exactly 5, 5, 5, 5. Probe: \emph{``Point at each dot as you
        say the number.''}
  \item \textbf{Reading the box as ``where the data is'' and the whiskers as empty} (1c, AP~1).
        Probe: \emph{``How many of the twenty are past the right edge of the box?''}
  \item \textbf{Two descriptions parked side by side} (2b, 2d, Application c, HW~4 --- the second
        misconception). Probe: \emph{``I read your sentence about Route A. Now cross out every
        word that is not comparing.''}
  \item \textbf{Comparison with no context} (2d, HW~4): ``the median is higher'' with no variable
        and no groups. Probe: \emph{``Higher what? Higher than what?''}
  \item \textbf{The lone dot read as an error in the data} (2c). It is a real rider with a real
        time. Probe: \emph{``Did somebody mistype it, or did somebody have a bad morning?''}
  \item \textbf{Shape claimed from a boxplot} (AP~4, HW~2): a boxplot cannot show a second peak.
        Probe: \emph{``Where inside that box would a second hump be drawn?''}
\end{itemize}
Cold-call prompts: \emph{``What fraction of the data is in the left whisker?''} \quad
\emph{``Give me a comparison sentence with a comparative word and a unit in it.''}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{teachernote}[Warm-Up]
Four minutes total, and item~1 is the one that must be right --- if a student cannot produce
$Q_1$ and $Q_3$ from nine ordered values, problem~1a will stall the whole group. Circulate during
item~1 specifically and fix quartile technique on the spot. Item~2 is a 60-second drill; do not
let it expand. \textbf{Put item~3's four lines of arithmetic on the board and leave them there
for the whole period} --- problem~2c is meant to \emph{recover} that rule, and having it visible
turns 2c from a memory test into a reasoning task, which is the point. \textbf{Do not say
\emph{five-number summary}, \emph{boxplot}, \emph{box}, or \emph{whisker} in this debrief.}
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Experience \& Formalize]
\textbf{Activity (20 min).} Roughly 11 minutes on problem~1, 9 on problem~2. This period merges
two CED topics, so the clock is real: at the 11-minute mark, move every group to problem~2 even
if 1d is unfinished --- 1d is recoverable in the debrief, and problem~2 is where Topic 1.9 lives.

\textbf{Make every group actually count in 1b.} Groups that estimate get ``about 4, about 7'' and
the discovery evaporates. The four exact fives are the entire lesson. If a group is stuck on
where the boundaries fall, send them back to their own 1a table rather than telling them.

\textbf{The single discipline that makes this lesson work: do not name anything while
circulating.} A group that hears ``whisker'' stops discovering.

\textbf{Debrief (12 min).} Must-land: step~3 (the crux, on their own count row) and step~5 (the
comparison checklist). If time runs short, compress steps~6 and~7 into two spoken sentences ---
the mean/median rule returns in 1.7 and the boxplot's cost returns in the homework.

\textbf{Application (6 min).} Part (c) is the AP-scored form. If you only get through (a) and
(c), that is the right trade --- (b) is recoverable from HW~2.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[AP Practice]
\textbf{This page is not required and not scored} --- the graded homework is the section behind it
at the back of the packet. Work it as AP-format reps and go over it at the start of Lesson~1.7.
\textbf{MC item~1 is the one worth reading closely}: option (A) says more weekend shifts fall in
the widest stretch of the display, which is the target misconception dressed as an AP answer
choice. \textbf{MC item~4 is the second one to read}: its correct answer is that the display
cannot tell you whether the weekend tips are bimodal, and students who have decided a boxplot
``shows the shape'' will not even consider it. If either item goes badly for more than a handful,
spend three minutes on the counting picture from problem~1 before starting 1.7. On the free
response, part (c) is the highest-value part --- it is Skill 2.D in the exact form the exam asks
it, and it is scored on comparative language, not on arithmetic.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Homework]
\textbf{This is the graded page} --- collect it next class and score it. The eight minutes at the
end of the period are a \emph{start}, not a completion: put them on 1, 2, and~3 and let 4 and~5
go home. Item~1 is the diagnostic --- a student who matches the wrong plot has a quartile problem,
not a boxplot problem, and needs Lesson 1.5 retaught in two minutes rather than this lesson
repeated. Item~3's arithmetic is in a \texttt{work} block authored byte-identically in the blank
and the key; require the boundary to be written out, not just the verdict. \textbf{Score item~4
against the comparison standard}: no comparative word, no credit, however accurate the two
descriptions are. Item~5's justification sentence is where you find out whether the crux
survived the drive home.
\end{teachernote}
