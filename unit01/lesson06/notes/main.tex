\mainidea[The five]{Numbers} &
A \textbf{boxplot} draws only \blank{1.2cm} numbers and nothing else. Those five are the
\textbf{five-number summary}, in this order: the \blank{2.0cm}, \blank{0.9cm}, the
\blank{1.5cm}, \blank{0.9cm}, and the \blank{2.0cm}. \textbf{Twenty Free Throws:} each player on
a squad shot 100. Their made totals, in order:

\vspace{2pt}
{\centering
30 \quad 31 \quad 31 \quad 32 \quad 33 \quad 36 \quad 36 \quad 37 \quad 38 \quad 38 \quad
41 \quad 43 \quad 45 \quad 47 \quad 49 \quad 52 \quad 56 \quad 60 \quad 66 \quad 74
\par}
\\
& Twenty values, so the median is halfway between the 10th and the 11th, and each quartile is
the middle of its own half of \blank{1.0cm} values --- the technique from Lesson~1.5:
\begin{work}
Q_1 &= \tfrac{33+36}{2} = 34.5, \qquad \text{median} = \tfrac{38+41}{2} = 39.5, \qquad Q_3 = \tfrac{49+52}{2} = 50.5
\end{work}
A boxplot is that summary \blank{1.4cm} to scale on a number line: it keeps those five and
\blank{2.3cm} everything in between.
\\

% ── ROW 2: boxplot anatomy ───────────────────────────────────────────────────
\mainidea[Anatomy of a]{Boxplot} &
The \textbf{box} runs from \blank{0.9cm} to \blank{0.9cm}, so it spans the middle
\blank{0.9cm}\% of the data, and the line drawn inside it is the \blank{1.5cm}. Each
\textbf{whisker} then runs from a quartile out to the most extreme value that is
\blank{2.8cm}. An outlier is never swallowed by a whisker --- it is plotted as
\blank{2.3cm}, and a display drawn that way is a \textbf{modified boxplot}, the version the
AP exam draws.
\\
& The box's width is the \textbf{IQR}, and the \blank{2.2cm} rule from Lesson~1.5 decides
where each whisker has to stop. Test both ends of the twenty free-throw totals:
\begin{work}
\text{IQR} &= 50.5 - 34.5 = 16 \text{ shots}, \qquad 34.5 - 1.5(16) = 10.5, \qquad 50.5 + 1.5(16) = 74.5
\end{work}
The lowest total is 30 and the highest is 74, so \blank{2.3cm} falls outside those two
boundaries and this display has \blank{2.1cm} --- both whiskers reach the extremes.
\\
& \notesprompt{Use the free-throw display above.}

\pcell{5}{How many of the twenty players sit inside the box?}{1.1cm}{} &
\pcell{6}{Name one thing the dotplot shows that the boxplot cannot.}{1.1cm}{} \\ \hline
\end{probgrid}

% ── ROW 3: comparing two distributions on a shared axis ─────────────────────
\mainidea[Comparing two groups]{Parallel Boxplots} &
Two or more boxplots on one shared axis are \textbf{parallel} (side-by-side) boxplots. A
comparison addresses \blank{1.5cm}, \blank{2.3cm}, and unusual features --- all three, every
time, each carrying a \blank{2.3cm} word (\emph{higher than}, \emph{more variable than}) and each
stated \blank{2.1cm}. Describing one group and then describing the other is \emph{not} a
comparison and earns nothing. Under skew the mean leaves the median: skewed right pulls the mean
to the \blank{1.4cm} of the median, skewed left to the \blank{1.2cm}.
\\
& A commuter timed the same morning trip on two bus routes, in minutes.

\par\vspace{2pt}
Put the two middle halves side by side, and then test Route B's slowest morning:
\begin{work}
\text{IQR}_{A} &= 26 - 21 = 5 \text{ min} \quad \text{IQR}_{B} = 32 - 20 = 12 \text{ min} \quad 32 + 1.5(12) = 50 < 58
\end{work}
So the 58-minute morning is drawn on \blank{1.7cm}, and Route B's upper whisker stops at
\blank{1.0cm} minutes --- the slowest morning that is \blank{2.8cm}.

% ── ROW 4: THE CRUX — every section holds one quarter (last instruction row) ─
\mainidea[Every section holds]{One quarter} &
The five numbers cut a boxplot into \blank{1.2cm} sections --- lower whisker, lower half of the
box, upper half of the box, upper whisker --- and every one of them holds \blank{0.9cm}\% of
the data, however wide it happens to look. Width reports \blank{1.5cm}; it never reports
\blank{1.4cm}.
\par\vspace{3pt}

gap, so each count is exact.
