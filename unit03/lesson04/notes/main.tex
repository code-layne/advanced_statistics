% ── ROW 1: the parts and the four principles ─────────────────────────────────
\mainidea[What makes it an]{Experiment} &
In an experiment the researcher \blank{1.2cm} treatments. The \textbf{experimental units} are
the individuals that are \blank{1.8cm} the treatments (\emph{subjects} when they are people).
A \textbf{factor} is an explanatory variable whose levels are set on purpose; each level (or
combination of levels) is a \textbf{treatment}. The \textbf{response variable} is measured
\blank{1.2cm} the treatments.
\par\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Four principles of a well-designed experiment}\par\vspace{2pt}
\stepnum{1}\ \textbf{Comparison} of at least \blank{1.0cm} treatment groups (one may be a
\textbf{control group}).\par
\stepnum{2}\ \textbf{Random assignment}: \blank{1.4cm} decides which unit gets which treatment,
so \textbf{confounding variables} tend to balance out across the groups.\par
\stepnum{3}\ \textbf{Replication}: more than \blank{1.0cm} unit in each treatment group.\par
\stepnum{4}\ \textbf{Control}: keep every other variable the \blank{1.3cm} for all groups.
\\
& A psychologist recruits 60 student volunteers. A computer randomly assigns 20 to chew mint
gum, 20 to chew fruit gum, and 20 to chew no gum while they study a list of 30 words. Then
she records how many words each student recalls.
\notesprompt{Name the parts, then the principles.}
\begin{probgrid}{|Y|Y|}
\pcell{1}{Name the experimental units and the response variable.}{1.3cm}{} &
\pcell{2}{Name the factor and the treatments.}{1.3cm}{} \\ \hline
\pcell{3}{Why should a computer, not the psychologist, decide who chews gum?}{1.5cm}{} &
\pcell{4}{The students know if they chew gum. Who could still be kept blind? Which kind of blinding is that?}{1.5cm}{} \\ \hline
\end{probgrid}
\\ \hline
% ── ROW 2: completely randomized vs randomized block — THE TRAP ──────────────
\mainidea[Two designs]{Block it?} &
In a \textbf{completely randomized design}, treatments are assigned to all the units
completely \blank{1.6cm}. In a \textbf{randomized block design}, units are first sorted into
\textbf{blocks} of units that are similar on a variable that affects the \blank{1.6cm}; then
treatments are assigned at random \blank{1.4cm} each block. Blocking takes the differences
between blocks out of the comparison of treatments.
\\

% ── ROW 3: matched pairs — THE CRUX ──────────────────────────────────────────
\mainidea[A block of two]{Matched pairs} &
A \textbf{matched pairs design} is a block design whose blocks have \blank{1.0cm} units.
Either pair up units that are alike on a variable related to the response, and let
\blank{1.4cm} decide which member of each pair gets which treatment; or give each unit
\blank{1.2cm} treatments, in a random \blank{1.2cm}.
\par\vspace{3pt}
A company tests a new shoe sole that may wear more slowly. Each of 15 runners wears one shoe
with the new sole and one with the old sole for a month; a coin flip decides which foot gets
the new sole. Then the tread wear on each shoe is measured.
\notesprompt{Pairs, order, and chance.}
\begin{probgrid}{|Y|Y|Y|}
\pcell{8}{Why is this better than giving 15 runners new soles and 15 others old soles?}{2.1cm}{} &
\pcell{9}{Each of 12 typists types once with music and once without. How should the order be decided? Why?}{2.1cm}{} &
\pcell{10}{``Each runner wears both soles, so the pairing controls everything. Skip the coin --- put the new sole on every right foot.'' Agree? Why?}{2.1cm}{} \\ \hline
\end{probgrid}
